\title{Linnik's constant below the zero-density exponent: $278/125$ from a zero-free half-plane}
\author{Ritchie Comley}
\date{Draft, 29 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We prove that there is a constant $K$ such that every reduced residue class modulo $q$ contains a prime
$p \le K q^{278/125}$ ($278/125 = 2.224$). The proof uses two recent results that have not been refereed: OpenAI's
zero-free half-plane $\operatorname{Re} s > 11/12$ for Dirichlet $L$-functions, and Chen, Gupta and Li's zero-density
estimate, which lowers the density exponent to $7/3$. From these the classical argument gives every exponent above
$7/3$. We go below the density exponent with Harman's sieve, fed by a Type~II estimate that sorts the characters by the
real parts of the zeros of their $L$-functions; the sieve's loss is bounded by a certificate computed in exact integer
arithmetic. The whole proof, including proofs of both inputs, is formalised in Lean~4 with Mathlib and depends only on
Lean's standard axioms. The exponent is to be compared with $7/3$, not with Xylouris's $5$, whose proof uses refereed
results only. With the classical density estimates of Ingham and Huxley in place of Chen, Gupta and Li's, the same
method gives about $2.27$ in place of $12/5$, by a computation that is not formalised.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
There is $K$ such that for every $q \ge 1$ and every $a$ prime to $q$ there is a prime $p \equiv a \pmod q$ with
$p \le K q^{278/125}$.
\end{theorem}

Linnik \cite{linnik44, linnik44b} proved that there are absolute constants $K$ and $L$ such that every reduced residue
class mod $q$ contains a prime $p \le K q^L$. The admissible exponent $L$, Linnik's constant, has been lowered many
times: Heath-Brown \cite{heath-brown92} reached $L = 5.5$, and Xylouris $L = 5.18$ \cite{xylouris-acta} and then
$L = 5$ \cite{xylouris11, xylouris18}. Under the generalised Riemann hypothesis every $L > 2$ is admissible
\cite{lls, lls-corr}. Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has $L = 278/125 = 2.224$, but it is not on the same footing as these
exponents, whose proofs use refereed results only: its proof uses two recent results that have not been refereed
(Section~\ref{sec:inputs}), and with them the classical argument already gives every $L > 7/3$. What this paper adds is
measured from $7/3$.

\subsection*{The zeros near $\operatorname{Re} s = 1$}

The difficulty in Linnik's theorem is the zeros of the $L$-functions mod $q$ near the line $\operatorname{Re} s = 1$.
By the explicit formula, a zero $\rho = \beta + i\gamma$ of $L(s, \chi)$ contributes about $x^{\beta - 1}$, relative to
the main term, to the count of primes $p \le x$ in a class mod $q$. A zero-density estimate with exponent $A$, a
bound of about $(qT)^{A(1 - \sigma)}$ for the number of zeros with real part at least $\sigma$, makes the zeros of
real part about $\sigma$ contribute about $(q^A/x)^{1 - \sigma}$ together. Classically the zero-free region allows
$1 - \beta$ as small as $c/\log q$, and one exceptional real zero may be closer still; at $x = q^L$ such a zero
contributes a constant, not a small quantity. The record proofs control these zeros with zero-free regions, density
estimates without logarithmic factors and the Deuring--Heilbronn phenomenon, and the constants of all three enter the
exponent.

The zero-free half-plane of OpenAI's release \cite{openai-main, openai-companion}, Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}, removes
the zeros with $\beta > 11/12$. Every remaining zero then contributes at most a power $x^{-1/12}$, and the problem
becomes one of density: a density exponent $A \ge 2$ for $1/2 \le \sigma \le 11/12$ gives Linnik's theorem with
every exponent $L > A$. Huxley's density estimate \cite{huxley75} gives $A = 12/5$. Chen, Gupta and Li \cite{cgl}, whose
method is Guth and Maynard's \cite{guth-maynard}, prove $A = 7/3$, uniformly in $q$ and $T$: their Theorem~1.2 bounds
the count, for a divisor $q_1$ of $q$, by four terms, and at $q_1 = q$ the largest of them on $[7/10, 4/5]$ has the
exponent $4/(1 + \sigma)$, which is $7/3$ at $\sigma = 5/7$ and smaller beyond; below $5/7$ Ingham's exponent
$3/(2 - \sigma)$ is at most $7/3$. With Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} the classical deduction turns the density exponent
$7/3$ into every Linnik exponent above $7/3$ (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}). Neither that deduction nor the exponent
of this paper needs the value $11/12$: what they use is a zero-free half-plane $\operatorname{Re} s > 1 - \eta_0$ for
some fixed $\eta_0 > 0$, so no zeros near $\operatorname{Re} s = 1$ and no exceptional zero
(Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).

\subsection*{Why a sieve can go below the density exponent}

For primes in short intervals a density exponent $A$ gives intervals of length $x^{1 - 1/A}$, and Harman's sieve goes
further, to $x^{0.525}$ \cite{bhp}. The same happens here. The sieve needs a \emph{Type~II estimate}: a bound for
bilinear sums $\sum_\chi |W(\chi)| |S(\chi)|$ over the non-trivial characters mod $q$, with $W$ a Dirichlet polynomial
of length $P = q^\mu$ with arbitrary coefficients and $S$ a product of prime sums of total length $x/P$. Sort the
characters by the largest real part $\beta$ of the zeros of their $L$-functions. Each prime sum is then at most about
$(x/P)^\beta$, and a density estimate with exponent $A$ bounds the number of characters by $q^{A(1 - \beta)}$.
Cauchy--Schwarz over these characters and orthogonality bound $\sum |W(\chi)|$ by the square root of that number times
$((q + P)P)^{1/2}$, and the square root is the gain: it halves the density exponent. Neglecting logarithms and margins,
the sum is then smaller than the main term when $(L - \mu - A/2)(1 - \beta) \ge (1 - \mu)/2$ if $\mu \le 1$, and when
$\mu \le L - A/2$ if $\mu \ge 1$. At $\mu = 1$, that is $P = q$, both conditions read $L \ge 1 + A/2$, whatever
$\beta$. So the Type~II estimate holds on a window of lengths around $P = q$ exactly when $L$ exceeds $1 + A/2$ for the
largest density exponent $A$. With $A = 7/3$ this threshold is $13/6 \approx 2.167$, against $7/3$ for the classical
argument.

The sieve needs a window of positive width, and it pays for the sums it cannot compare. At $L = 278/125$ the window is
$[u_1, u_2] = [0.41556, 0.47531]$ in exponents of $x = q^L$ (Section~\ref{sec:type2}), and the primes that the sieve
loses, bounded by a finite computation, are fewer than all of them (Section~\ref{sec:certificate}). The table compares
what the inputs give with and without the sieve (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}).

\subsection*{Harman's sieve, and what is new}

Harman's sieve \cite{harman83, harman} bounds from below the number of primes in the set $\mathcal A$ of integers
$n \in (x, 2x]$ with $n \equiv a \pmod q$ by comparing it with the set $\mathcal B$ of the integers $n \in (x, 2x]$
prime to $q$, weighted by $1/\varphi(q)$. It needs two kinds of information. Type~I information is the comparison of
$\mathcal A$ with $\mathcal B$ on the multiples of each integer prime to $q$, which counting in residue classes gives.
Type~II information is the comparison on bilinear sums $\sum_{mn} a_m b_n$, with $m$ in the window's range
$(x^{u_1}, x^{u_2})$, bounded coefficients $a_m$, and $b_n$ supported on products of primes, which the Type~II
estimate gives. The sieve recovers the primes from the sums it can compare, and a finite computation bounds the
fraction it loses.

The paper's contributions are three.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A Type~II estimate over the zeros' real parts} (Section~\ref{sec:type2}). The characters mod $q$ are
  sorted into bins by the largest real part of the zeros of their $L$-functions up to a height. In each bin the
  bilinear sum is bounded by the zeros, which make the Dirichlet polynomial of the primes small, and by the number
  of characters in the bin, which a density estimate bounds; the better of orthogonality and the
  Hal\'asz--Montgomery inequality is taken in each bin. Inputs~\ref{inp:half-plane} and~\ref{inp:density},
  with the classical density estimates of Ingham and Huxley, give the window
  $[u_1, u_2] = [41556/100000,\ 47531/100000]$ at $L = 278/125$.
\item \emph{A decomposition by Buchstab's identity, with a refinement} (Section~\ref{sec:certificate}). The
  identity is applied to depth six, the primes of the two deepest levels on a coarser grid. The terms in which the
  product of some of the prime variables has its exponent in the window, or in its reflection $[1 - u_2, 1 - u_1]$,
  are Type~II sums and are evaluated; of the others, a term with sign $-$ is decomposed further, and a term with sign
  $+$ is dropped or decomposed further. The refinement keeps, in a dropped term of depth two, the part whose integers
  have exactly two prime factors in the cofactor and are, in some grouping of their four prime factors, a Type~II sum.
\item \emph{A certificate} (Section~\ref{sec:certificate}). The fraction of the primes the decomposition loses is
  bounded above by a sum over the cells of a grid of the exponents, computed exactly in integers, with Buchstab's
  function itself where its argument lies in $[2, 3]$ and the trapezoid rule for the cells' prime sums. On a grid of
  $340$ cells a side (and $85$ cells of four for the deepest primes) the sum is $0.99754$, and Lean's kernel checks
  that it is below $0.99854$, so below $1$: a positive proportion of the primes remains.
\end{enumerate}

The rest is not new. Inputs~\ref{inp:half-plane} and~\ref{inp:density} are OpenAI's and Chen, Gupta and Li's,
and the density estimates of Ingham \cite{ingham} and Huxley \cite{huxley72, huxley75}, the Hal\'asz--Montgomery
inequality \cite{halasz, montgomery69}, Buchstab's identity \cite{buchstab}, Brun's sieve \cite{brun} and Harman's
sieve are classical.

\subsection*{Status and formalisation}

The Lean statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} has no hypotheses, and its axioms are Lean's standard three, so the
theorem is unconditional. Its proof uses two recent results that have not been refereed, OpenAI's zero-free
half-plane and Chen, Gupta and Li's zero-density estimate, which Section~\ref{sec:inputs} states as
Inputs~\ref{inp:half-plane} and~\ref{inp:density}. The library proves both, the half-plane along OpenAI's companion
preprint and the density estimate from Chen, Gupta and Li's Proposition~3.1, so the truth of the theorem rests on
Lean's kernel, Mathlib and the pinned toolchain, not on the preprints. Whether the Lean statements of the inputs
faithfully capture the preprints' theorems matters for attributing and interpreting them, not for the truth of the
theorem, and the kernel does not check it. Every step of the proof, and every result from the literature that it uses,
is a theorem of the Lean library \cite{repo}, written with Lean~4 \cite{lean4} and Mathlib \cite{mathlib}, or, for
Dirichlet's theorem, of Mathlib. Appendix~\ref{app:claims} names them, Section~\ref{sec:lean} says what is formalised
and what is known of independent checking of the inputs, and the directory \file{formalisations/} of the repository
documents each result from the literature: its source, its Lean statement and the differences between them. The proofs
of the paper are those of the library, written at the level of detail of a paper; where the Lean proof fixes a constant
whose value the argument does not need, the paper usually says only that the constant exists. The argument, the
formalisation and this text were developed with the help of AI systems (see the acknowledgements); the author is
responsible for the paper.

\subsection*{Related work}

Heath-Brown and Iwaniec \cite{hb-iwaniec} combined a sieve with zero-density estimates, which bound Dirichlet
polynomials over primes in the $t$-aspect; the bins of Section~\ref{sec:type2} are the analogue in the $q$-aspect,
over the characters mod $q$. Harman's sieve with a numerical bound for its loss is the method of Baker, Harman and
Pintz \cite{bhp} and of R.~Li \cite{li-harman}. R.~Li also adapts his calculation to moduli all of whose prime factors
are below $q^C$, under a zero-free region of Vinogradov's type, and obtains $1/0.4752 < 2.1044$ for the powers of a
fixed prime; Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} implies his hypothesis, so for such moduli his theorem, if it holds as stated,
gives a smaller exponent (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}). Theorem~\ref{thm:main} holds for every modulus.

\section{Notation and the two inputs}\label{sec:inputs}

Throughout, $q \ge 1$ is the modulus and $a$ is prime to $q$. The Dirichlet characters mod $q$ are $\chi$, the trivial
one is $\chi_0$, and $L(s, \chi)$ is the $L$-function of $\chi$, continued to the whole plane. For
$1/2 \le \sigma \le 1$ and $T \ge 2$ let
\[
  N^*(\sigma, T; q) = \#\{\chi \bmod q : L(s, \chi) = 0 \text{ for some } s \in \mathcal R(\sigma, T)\},
\]
where $\mathcal R(\sigma, T) = \{s : \operatorname{Re} s \ge \sigma,\ |\operatorname{Im} s| \le T\}$: the number of
characters mod $q$ whose $L$-function has a zero in the rectangle. It counts characters, not zeros, so a bound
for the number of zeros bounds it. The proof is a sieve on the integers of $(x, 2x]$, $x = q^L$, with $L = 278/125$ in
the end, and $q$ is taken large.

The paper states two inputs, written \emph{Input} and not \emph{hypothesis} because the library proves them
(Section~\ref{sec:lean}). A statement below that holds `under' an input is conditional in the text of the paper.

\begin{inputthm}[the zero-free half-plane]\label{inp:half-plane}
For every $q \ge 1$, every Dirichlet character $\chi$ mod $q$ and every $s$ with $\operatorname{Re} s > 11/12$,
$L(s, \chi) \ne 0$, except at $s = 1$ for the trivial character.
\end{inputthm}

The exception is the pole of $L(s, \chi_0)$ at $s = 1$. This is OpenAI's result: their companion preprint
\cite{openai-companion} proves it, and their main preprint \cite{openai-main} proves the stronger
$\operatorname{Re} s > 7/8$. The library proves it along the companion preprint's route (\S S5).

\begin{inputthm}[zero-density estimates from $5/7$ to $131/168$]\label{inp:density}
Let $\sigma_k = 5/7 + k/1680$ for $0 \le k \le 14$ and $\sigma_k = \sigma_{14} + (k - 14)/280$ for $14 \le k \le 30$, so
that $\sigma_{30} = 131/168$. There are exponents $A_k$, for $0 \le k < 30$, with
$4/(1 + \sigma_k) < A_k < 4/(1 + \sigma_k) + 1/10000$ such that for each $k$ there is $C$ with the following property:
for every $q \ge 1$, every $\sigma_k \le \sigma \le \sigma_{k+1}$ and every $T \ge 2$, at most
$C (qT)^{A_k (1 - \sigma)} (\log qT)^{10}$ characters mod $q$ have an $L$-function with a zero in
$\operatorname{Re} s \ge \sigma$, $|\operatorname{Im} s| \le T$.
\end{inputthm}

The exponent $A_k$ is the exponent $4/(1 + \sigma)$ of the first term of Chen, Gupta and Li's Theorem~1.2 \cite{cgl}
at $q_1 = q$, the largest of its four terms on $[7/10, 4/5]$, taken at the step's left end $\sigma_k$, up to
$10^{-4}$. The $A_k$ used here, multiples of $10^{-5}$ between $4/(1 + \sigma_k) + 3.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ and
$4/(1 + \sigma_k) + 8 \cdot 10^{-5}$, are
\[
\begin{array}{r@{\quad}llllll}
k = 0\text{--}5:   & 2.33340 & 2.33258 & 2.33178 & 2.33096 & 2.33016 & 2.32934 \\
k = 6\text{--}11:  & 2.32854 & 2.32772 & 2.32692 & 2.32610 & 2.32532 & 2.32452 \\
k = 12\text{--}17: & 2.32370 & 2.32290 & 2.32212 & 2.31728 & 2.31252 & 2.30774 \\
k = 18\text{--}23: & 2.30300 & 2.29826 & 2.29358 & 2.28889 & 2.28423 & 2.27959 \\
k = 24\text{--}29: & 2.27493 & 2.27035 & 2.26575 & 2.26117 & 2.25661 & 2.25207
\end{array}
\]
Proposition~\ref{prop:window} holds for these values, and for every other choice that Input~\ref{inp:density}
allows. Their estimate counts zeros, which gives the count of characters stated here. The library derives each step
from their Proposition~3.1, the large-values estimate, in the form for one point per character that their proof uses
(distinct primitive characters, each with one height, and sums over $[6N/5, 9N/5]$ with $N \ge (qT)^{2/3}/2$ and
$7/10 \le \sigma \le 4/5$); it does not prove their Theorem~1.2 as they state it (\S S5).

\subsection*{The density curve}

The Type~II estimate of Section~\ref{sec:type2} needs a bound for $N^*(\sigma, T; q)$ at every $\sigma$ in
$[1/2, 1]$. It takes it in the form of a curve.

\begin{definition}\label{def:curve}
A function $g : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is a \emph{density curve} if there is $C \ge 0$ such that for every
$q \ge 1$, every $1/2 \le \sigma \le 1$ and every $T \ge 2$,
\[
  N^*(\sigma, T; q) \le C\, (qT)^{g(\sigma)} \bigl(1 + \log qT\bigr)^{10}.
\]
\end{definition}

The curve of this paper takes the trivial count below $4/7$, Ingham's
$3/(2 - \sigma)$ up to $5/7$, Chen, Gupta and Li's $4/(1 + \sigma)$ up to $131/168$, Huxley's $3/(3\sigma - 1)$ up to
$5/6$, and a constant exponent above, where Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} removes the zeros beyond $11/12$. The window
condition of Section~\ref{sec:type2} is checked piece by piece, on pieces of the form $A(1 - \sigma)$, so the exponents
are frozen on short steps and rounded up. The curve $g^*$ is the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item $g^*(\sigma) = 1$ for $\sigma < 4/7$.
\item On $[4/7, 5/7)$, divided into twenty steps $[\sigma_-, \sigma_+)$ of length $1/140$,
  $g^*(\sigma) = \bigl(3/(2 - \sigma_+) + 1/2000\bigr)(1 - \sigma)$: Ingham's exponent at the step's right end plus an
  excess of $1/2000$, except on the last step $[99/140, 5/7)$, where the excess is $1/100000$ and the exponent is
  $7/3 + 1/100000$.
\item On $[5/7, 131/168)$, divided into the $30$ steps $[\sigma_k, \sigma_{k+1})$ of Input~\ref{inp:density},
  $g^*(\sigma) = A_k(1 - \sigma)$.
\item On $[131/168, 5/6)$, divided into fifteen steps $[\sigma_-, \sigma_+)$ of length $1/280$,
  $g^*(\sigma) = \bigl(3/(3\sigma_- - 1) + 1/2000\bigr)(1 - \sigma)$: Huxley's exponent at the step's left end plus
  $1/2000$.
\item $g^*(\sigma) = (101/50)(1 - \sigma)$ for $\sigma \ge 5/6$.
\end{itemize}
The exponent rises from about $2.111$ to $7/3 + 1/100000$ on $[4/7, 5/7]$, is $2.3334$ on the first step after $5/7$,
and then falls, to about $2.015$ at $5/6$.
On each step $A(1 - \sigma) \le A(1 - \sigma_-) \le 1$, so $g^* \le 1$ everywhere, and $g^*(1/2) = 1$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:curve}
Under Inputs~\ref{inp:half-plane} and~\ref{inp:density}, $g^*$ is a density curve.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A finite union of ranges on each of which the bound holds, with its own constant, is a range on which it holds with
the sum of the constants, so it suffices to prove the bound on each piece.
\begin{itemize}
\item For $\sigma < 4/7$: $N^*(\sigma, T; q) \le \varphi(q) \le qT$.
\item On each step of $[4/7, 5/7]$: Ingham's estimate \cite{ingham} in the form over the characters mod $q$, which
  bounds $N^*(\sigma, T; q)$ by $C(qT)^{A(1 - \sigma)}(\log qT)^{10}$ on $[\sigma_-, \sigma_+]$ for every $A$
  above $3/(2 - \sigma_+)$. Ingham proved his estimate for the zeros of
  $\zeta$; the form over the characters is Theorem~12.1 of Montgomery's book \cite{montgomery-topics} (as cited by
  Huxley \cite{huxley75}).
\item On each step of $[5/7, 131/168]$: Input~\ref{inp:density}.
\item On each step of $[131/168, 5/6]$: Huxley's estimate \cite{huxley75} over the characters mod $q$, with the
  exponent $3/(3\sigma_- - 1) + 1/2000$ on $[\sigma_-, \sigma_+]$. His
  (1.1) is a bound $(Q^2 T/q_0)^{12(1 - \sigma)/5 + \varepsilon}$ for the zeros of the $L$-functions of the primitive
  characters to the moduli up to $Q$ divisible by $q_0$, and his (2.23) has the exponent $3/(3\sigma - 1)$ there;
  with $Q = q_0 = d$ for each $d \mid q$, the case of one modulus, it bounds the characters mod $q$, each of which is
  induced by a primitive character mod some $d \mid q$ with the same zeros in $\operatorname{Re} s > 0$, at a cost
  $\tau(q) \ll q^\varepsilon$. The excess $1/2000$ absorbs this $q^\varepsilon$ and Huxley's own $\varepsilon$,
  since $(1 - \sigma)/2000 \ge 1/12000$ on these steps.
\item For $\sigma \ge 5/6$: the exponent $101/50$. On $[5/6, 11/12]$ Huxley's exponent $3/(3\sigma - 1)$ is at most
  $2$, and his zero-detection argument, with its parameters fixed for this range, gives $2 + 1/50$; above $11/12$ at
  most the trivial character is counted, by Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}.
\end{itemize}
Each of these bounds has a factor $(\log qT)^{10}$, which is at most $(1 + \log qT)^{10}$.
\end{proof}

Two pieces of $g^*$ are not classical. One is the steps of Input~\ref{inp:density} on $[5/7, 131/168]$: their
exponents start just above $7/3$ ($2.3334$) at $5/7$ and decrease like $4/(1 + \sigma)$, and they replace the classical
exponent $\min(3/(2 - \sigma), 3/(3\sigma - 1))$, which is Ingham's up to $\sigma = 3/4$, where both equal $12/5$, and
Huxley's after. The other is the part above $11/12$, which Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} gives. Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}
also enters the Type~II estimate (Section~\ref{sec:type2}) and, in the formalisation, the prime number theorem
(Section~\ref{sec:proof}).

\section{The Type II estimate}\label{sec:type2}

Fix a smooth real function $f_0$ supported in $[1/2, 2]$. For a character $\chi$, a scale $Q \ge 4$ and a
height $h \in \mathbb R$, the smoothed prime sum is
\[
  S_Q(\chi, h) = \sum_{p \le 2Q} \chi(p) \Bigl(\frac{p}{Q}\Bigr)^{-ih} f_0\Bigl(\frac{p}{Q}\Bigr),
\]
and for a finite set $S \subseteq (P, 2P]$ of integers, coefficients $a_m \in \mathbb C$ and a height $h_0$,
\[
  W(\chi) = \sum_{m \in S} a_m \chi(m) m^{-ih_0}.
\]
The sieve of Section~\ref{sec:type1} needs, for the integers with a factor $m$ in a range $(x^{u_1}, x^{u_2})$ and
the rest a product of primes, that the non-trivial characters contribute less than the main term. In the Dirichlet
polynomials above this is the following estimate. Write $\mathcal X^*$ for the non-trivial characters mod $q$.

\begin{theorem}[the Type~II estimate]\label{thm:type2}
Assume Inputs~\ref{inp:half-plane} and~\ref{inp:density}, and let $L = 278/125$, $u_1 = 41556/100000$ and
$u_2 = 47531/100000$. Let
$K_0 \ge 0$ be an integer, $e_1 > 0$ and $0 < \eta \le 10^{-5}$. There is $q_0$, depending on $f_0$, $K_0$, $e_1$ and
$\eta$, such that for every $q \ge q_0$, every
integer $P$ with $q^{u_1 L} \le P \le q^{u_2 L}$, every $S \subseteq (P, 2P]$, all $a_m$ and $h_0$, every $K \le K_0$,
and all $Q_1, \dots, Q_K \ge q^{e_1}$ and $|h_1|, \dots, |h_K| \le q^\eta$ with $Q_1 \cdots Q_K \le 2^{K_0} q^L/P$,
\[
  \sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X^*} |W(\chi)| \prod_{j=1}^K |S_{Q_j}(\chi, h_j)|
  \le q^{L - 1/50000} \Bigl(\frac1P \sum_{m \in S} |a_m|^2\Bigr)^{1/2}.
\]
\end{theorem}

At $x = q^L$ the condition on $P$ is $x^{u_1} \le P \le x^{u_2}$. By Cauchy--Schwarz and
$Q_1 \cdots Q_K \le 2^{K_0} q^L/P$, the trivial character's term is at most a constant times
$q^L (\sum |a_m|^2/P)^{1/2}$, and the bound saves the power $q^{-1/50000}$ against it.
The proof has three parts: the prime sums are bounded through the zeros of each character's $L$-function, the
characters are sorted by those zeros, and in each class the sum over the characters is bounded in two ways.
Sections~\ref{sec:window} to~\ref{sec:window-278} give the condition on the window under which this works, where
it comes from, and its check at $L = 278/125$.

\subsection{Prime sums in a zero-free rectangle}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:prime-sum}
Assume Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}. For every integer $J \ge 0$ there are $B, C \ge 0$ such that the following holds
for every $q \ge 1$, every
$\chi \in \mathcal X^*$, every $1/2 \le \theta \le 11/12$, $0 < \delta \le 1/24$, $H \ge 0$, $Q \ge 4$ and
$y \in \mathbb R$. If $L(s, \chi) \ne 0$ for $\operatorname{Re} s > \theta$, $|\operatorname{Im} s - y| < H + 1$,
then
\begin{multline*}
  |S_Q(\chi, y)| \le M_\delta(\theta)\, \lambda\, B\,
  \frac{Q^{\theta + \delta} + Q^{11/12 + \delta}(1 + H)^{-J}}{\log Q} \\
  + C\bigl(\sqrt{2Q} + 1\bigr)\bigl(\log_2 (2Q) + 1\bigr),
\end{multline*}
where $\lambda = \log(q + 2) + \log(|y| + 4) + 1$ and $M_\delta(\theta) = C_L(\theta, \theta + \delta) +
C_L(11/12, 11/12 + \delta)$, with $C_L$ the constant of the bound for $L'/L$ in \S S1, depends only on $\theta$
and $\delta$.
\end{lemma}

The argument is the classical one for primes in progressions \cite{iwaniec-kowalski}, with a smooth weight in place
of a sharp cut-off:
Perron's formula for $-L'/L$ against the Mellin transform of the weight, a bound for $L'/L$ in zero-free discs by a
lemma of Landau's type, and a contour that runs along $\operatorname{Re} s = \theta + \delta$ at the heights near $y$
and along $\operatorname{Re} s = 11/12 + \delta$ elsewhere. The only use of Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} is to move the
line of integration to $\operatorname{Re} s = 11/12 + \delta$ and to bound $L'/L$ on that line and to its right.
\S S1 gives the proof.

\subsection{The bins}\label{sec:bins}

Let $I = 500000$ and $\beta_i = 1/2 + 5i/(12I)$ for $0 \le i \le I$, so that $\beta_0 = 1/2$, $\beta_I = 11/12$ and
the bins have width $1/1200000$. For a height bound $T \ge 2$ the \emph{bin} of $\chi \in \mathcal X^*$ is the largest
$i < I$ such that $L(s, \chi)$ has a zero with $\operatorname{Re} s > \beta_i$ and $|\operatorname{Im} s| \le T$, and
$0$ if there is none. If $\chi$ is in the bin $i$ then:
\begin{itemize}
\item $L(s, \chi)$ has no zero with $\operatorname{Re} s > \beta_{i+1}$ and $|\operatorname{Im} s| \le T$: for
  $i + 1 < I$ by the choice of $i$, and for $i + 1 = I$ by Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}. So
  Lemma~\ref{lem:prime-sum} applies with $\theta = \beta_{i+1}$ to every height $|y| \le T - H - 1$.
\item For $i \ge 1$, $\chi$ is counted by $N^*(\beta_i, T; q)$, so a density curve $g$ bounds the number of characters
  in the bin by $C(qT)^{g(\beta_i)}(1 + \log qT)^{10}$. For $i = 0$ the bound $\varphi(q) \le qT$ is the same, with
  $g(1/2) = 1$ and $\max(C, 1)$ in place of $C$, which the bound for every $i$ then uses.
\end{itemize}
The bins depend on $T$, and so on all the zeros up to the height $T$, but not on the heights $h_j$. The proof takes
$\delta = 10^{-6}$, $H = q^\eta$ and $T = 2H + 1 \le 3q^\eta$, so that every height $|h_j| \le q^\eta$ has
$|h_j| + H + 1 \le T$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:prime-sum} with $J = \lceil 5(1 + L)/(12\eta) \rceil$. As
$Q_j \ge q^{e_1} \ge 4$ for large $q$ and $Q_1 \cdots Q_K \le 2^{K_0} q^L/P$, each $Q_j \le q^{1 + L}$ for large $q$, so that
$Q_j^{11/12 + \delta}(1 + H)^{-J} \le Q_j^{1/2 + \delta}$. The hypothesis $Q_j \ge q^{e_1}$ gives
$\lambda/\log Q_j \ll 1/e_1$, and $M_\delta$ is bounded on the $I + 1$ values $\beta_i$. So for large $q$,
$|S_{Q_j}(\chi, h_j)| \le 2 Q_j^{\beta_{i+1} + 2\delta}$ in the bin $i$, and the product of the prime sums is at most
$4^{K_0} (q^L/P)^{\beta_i + \varpi}$ with $\varpi = 1/1200000 + 2\delta < 2.9 \cdot 10^{-6}$. So the density curve is
used only for the zeros up to the height $T \le 3q^\eta$, a small power of $q$.

\subsection{Two bounds in a bin}\label{sec:two-bounds}

Let $\mathcal X_i$ be the set of the characters in the bin $i$. Since the prime sums are bounded on $\mathcal X_i$,
it remains to bound $\sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X_i} |W(\chi)|$. There are two bounds.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Orthogonality.} For $S \subseteq (P, 2P]$ and every $t$,
  \[
    \sum_\chi \Bigl|\sum_{m \in S} c_m \chi(m) m^{-it}\Bigr|^2 \le (P + q) \sum_{m \in S} |c_m|^2,
  \]
  summed over all characters mod $q$. Indeed the left side is $\varphi(q)$ times the sum, over the classes mod $q$
  prime to $q$, of the squared moduli of the sums over the classes; each class has at most $P/q + 1$ elements in $(P, 2P]$, and
  Cauchy--Schwarz in each class and $\varphi(q) \le q$ give the bound. With Cauchy--Schwarz over $\mathcal X_i$,
  \[
    \sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X_i} |W(\chi)| \le |\mathcal X_i|^{1/2} \Bigl((P + q) \sum_{m \in S} |a_m|^2\Bigr)^{1/2}.
  \]
\item \emph{The Hal\'asz--Montgomery inequality} \cite{halasz, montgomery69}, in the form over the characters mod
  $q$ at one height:
  \[
    \Bigl(\sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X_i} |W(\chi)|\Bigr)^2 \le e \sum_{m \in S} |a_m|^2 \cdot
    \bigl(2P |\mathcal X_i| + |\mathcal X_i|^2 \mathcal H\bigr),
  \]
  where $\mathcal H$ bounds $|k_{2P}(\psi)|$ for every non-trivial character $\psi$ mod $q$, with
  $k_Y(\psi) = \sum_{n \ge 1} \psi(n) e^{-n/Y}$. Indeed, with $|c_\chi| \le 1$ and
  $c_\chi W(\chi) = |W(\chi)|$, $\sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X_i} |W(\chi)| = \sum_m a_m m^{-ih_0}
  \sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X_i} c_\chi \chi(m)$; Cauchy--Schwarz with the weight
  $e^{-m/2P}$, which is at least $e^{-1}$ on $(P, 2P]$, and extending the second sum to all $m \ge 1$ give
  $(\sum_\chi |W(\chi)|)^2 \le e \sum_m |a_m|^2 \sum_{\chi, \chi'} c_\chi \overline{c_{\chi'}}
  k_{2P}(\chi \overline{\chi'})$. The diagonal terms are at most $\sum_{n \ge 1} e^{-n/2P} \le 2P$, and the
  others at most $\mathcal H$, since $\chi \overline{\chi'}$ is non-trivial for $\chi \ne \chi'$.
\end{enumerate}
For the kernels, partial summation and the Pólya--Vinogradov inequality \cite{davenport} give, for every non-trivial
character $\psi$ mod $q$ and every $Y > 0$,
\[
  |k_Y(\psi)| \le \max_N \Bigl|\sum_{n \le N} \psi(n)\Bigr| \ll_\varepsilon q^{1/2 + \varepsilon},
\]
since the weight $e^{-n/Y}$ decreases from below $1$ to $0$. This is all that the window needs
(Section~\ref{sec:window-278}). The window condition of Section~\ref{sec:window} is written, like the formalisation,
for a sharper bound, which depends on $Y$ and follows from Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}. Let
$\delta' = \varepsilon' = 1/1000$. There is $C$ such that for every $q$, every non-trivial character $\psi$ mod $q$ and
every $Y \ge 1$,
\[
  |k_Y(\psi)| \le C Y^{1/12 - \delta'} (2q)^{5/12 + \delta' + 2\varepsilon'}.
\]
By Mellin inversion, $k_Y(\psi) = (2\pi i)^{-1} \int_{(2)} L(s, \psi) \Gamma(s) Y^s \, ds$. Since $L(\cdot, \psi)$ is
entire and $1/12 - \delta' > 0$, the line moves to $\operatorname{Re} s = 1/12 - \delta'$ without crossing a pole. There
the functional equation of the primitive character $\psi^*$ that induces $\psi$, of conductor $q^*$, gives
$|L(s, \psi^*)| \ll (q^*(|\operatorname{Im} s| + 2))^{5/12 + \delta'} |L(1 - s, \overline{\psi^*})|$, and $1 - s$ lies
on $\operatorname{Re} s = 11/12 + \delta'$. There Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} gives the Lindelöf-type bound
$|L(s, \chi)|^{\pm 1} \ll ((q + 2)(|\operatorname{Im} s| + 4))^{\varepsilon'}$, uniformly in $q$ and $\chi \ne \chi_0$:
Landau's lemma, as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:prime-sum}, bounds $|L'/L(s, \chi)|$ by a multiple of
$\log((q + 2)(|\operatorname{Im} s| + 4))$ on $\operatorname{Re} s \ge 11/12 + \delta'/2$; the three-lines theorem, on
rectangles of height comparable to that logarithm, interpolates this against $|L'/L| \ll 1$ on
$\operatorname{Re} s = 3/2$; and integrating $L'/L$ from $\operatorname{Re} s = 3/2$, where
$|\log |L|| \le \log \zeta(3/2)$, gives the bound for $|L|$ and $1/|L|$. The Euler factors at the primes dividing $q$
are at most $2$ each, which is $\ll q^{\varepsilon'}$ in all, and together
$|L(s, \psi)| \ll (q(|\operatorname{Im} s| + 2))^{5/12 + \delta' + 2\varepsilon'}$ on the new line; the decay of
$\Gamma$ makes the integral converge. At $Y = 2P$ this gives
$\mathcal H = C(2P)^{1/12 - \delta'}(2q)^{5/12 + \delta' + 2\varepsilon'}$.

The first bound is the better one when the bin is large, the second when it is small, which is the case for $\beta$
near $11/12$, on the top piece $[5/6, 11/12]$ of the curve. The proof takes the better of the two in each bin.

\subsection{The window condition}\label{sec:window}

Write $P = q^\mu$, and let $\beta = \beta_i$. Since $T \le 3q^\eta$ and $g \le 1$, the bin has
$|\mathcal X_i| \le 3C q^{g(\beta) + \eta}(1 + \log qT)^{10}$, and the product of the prime sums is at most
$4^{K_0} q^{(L - \mu)(\beta + \varpi)}$. Up to constants, powers of $1 + \log qT$ and the factor $q^\eta$, the two
bounds are $q^E (\sum |a_m|^2/P)^{1/2}$ with the exponents
\[
  E_1 = (L - \mu)(\beta + \varpi) + \frac{g(\beta)}2 + \frac\mu2 + \frac{\max(1, \mu)}2
\]
by orthogonality, and, by the Hal\'asz--Montgomery inequality with
$(2P|\mathcal X_i| + |\mathcal X_i|^2 \mathcal H)^{1/2} \le (2P|\mathcal X_i|)^{1/2} + |\mathcal X_i| \mathcal H^{1/2}$,
the larger of
\begin{align*}
  E_2 &= (L - \mu)(\beta + \varpi) + \frac{g(\beta)}2 + \mu, \\
  E_3 &= (L - \mu)(\beta + \varpi) + \frac{g(\beta)}2 + \frac\mu2 + \frac{g(\beta) + b(\mu)}2,
\end{align*}
with $b(\mu) = 5/12 + \mu/12 + \delta'(1 - \mu) + 2\varepsilon'$ the exponent of $\mathcal H$. For $\mu \ge 1$,
$E_2 = E_1$, so the Hal\'asz--Montgomery inequality never helps there.

In this section a window is an interval $[\mu_1, \mu_2]$ of exponents of $q$, for the scale $P = q^\mu$ of the Type~II
variable; the other sections write it $[u_1, u_2]$, in the exponents of $x = q^L$, so that $\mu_1 = u_1 L$ and $\mu_2 = u_2 L$.

\begin{definition}\label{def:window}
A window $[\mu_1, \mu_2]$ is \emph{admissible} for a curve $g$ at $L$ if for every $1/2 \le \beta \le 11/12$, every
$\mu_1 \le \mu \le \mu_2$ and every $0 \le \varpi \le 4 \cdot 10^{-6}$, with $\delta' = \varepsilon' = 1/1000$,
either $E_1 \le L - 1/25000$, or $\mu \le 1$ and $\max(E_2, E_3) \le L - 1/25000$.
\end{definition}

So at each point one of the two bounds has an exponent at most $L - 1/25000$.

\begin{lemma}[one bin]\label{lem:bin}
Let $g \le 1$ be a function and $[\mu_1, \mu_2]$ a window admissible for $g$ at $L$. Given $C_0, C_d, C_K \ge 0$, there
is $C'$ such that the following holds. Let $q > 1$, $\mu_1 \le \mu \le \mu_2$, $P = q^\mu$, $1 \le T \le 3q^\eta$
with $\eta \ge 0$, $1/2 \le \beta \le 11/12$ and $0 \le \varpi \le 4 \cdot 10^{-6}$. Let $\mathcal X$ be a set of at
most $C_d (qT)^{g(\beta)} (1 + \log qT)^{10}$ characters on which
$\prod_{j=1}^K |S_{Q_j}(\chi, h_j)| \le C_0 (q^L/P)^{\beta + \varpi}$, for some $K$, $Q_j$ and $h_j$, and let
$\mathcal H \le C_K (2P)^{1/12 - \delta'} (2q)^{5/12 + \delta' + 2\varepsilon'}$ bound the off-diagonal kernels on
$\mathcal X$. Then for every $S \subseteq (P, 2P]$ and all $a_m$ and $h_0$,
\[
  \sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X} |W(\chi)| \prod_{j=1}^K |S_{Q_j}(\chi, h_j)|
  \le C' (1 + \log qT)^{10} q^{L - 1/25000 + \eta} \Bigl(\frac1P \sum_{m \in S} |a_m|^2\Bigr)^{1/2}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The left side is at most $C_0 (q^L/P)^{\beta + \varpi} \sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X} |W(\chi)|$, and the two bounds of
Section~\ref{sec:two-bounds} bound the sum. At $(\beta, \mu, \varpi)$ the window condition gives $E_1 \le L - 1/25000$ or $\max(E_2, E_3) \le L - 1/25000$. In the
first case, the orthogonality bound with $(P + q)^{1/2} \le \sqrt2\, q^{\max(1, \mu)/2}$ and $|\mathcal X|^{1/2} \le
(3C_d)^{1/2} q^{(g(\beta) + \eta)/2}(1 + \log qT)^5$ gives the claim with the exponent $E_1 + \eta/2$. In the second, the
Hal\'asz--Montgomery bound, split as above, gives two terms: $(2P|\mathcal X|)^{1/2}$ carries the exponent
$E_2 + \eta/2$, and $|\mathcal X| \mathcal H^{1/2}$ the exponent $E_3 + \eta$, with $(1 + \log qT)^{10}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:type2-gen}
Assume Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}. Let $g$ be a density curve with $g \le 1$ and $g(1/2) = 1$, and let $[\mu_1, \mu_2]$ be
admissible for $g$ at $L$. Then the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2} holds with $q^{\mu_1} \le P \le q^{\mu_2}$ in
place of $q^{u_1 L} \le P \le q^{u_2 L}$, with $L$ in place of $278/125$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the bins of Section~\ref{sec:bins} and the kernel bound of Section~\ref{sec:two-bounds}, each bin $i$ satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma~\ref{lem:bin} at
$\beta = \beta_i$ and $\varpi = 1/1200000 + 2\delta$, with constants that do not depend on $q$, so each contributes at
most $C' (1 + \log qT)^{10} q^{L - 1/25000 + \eta}(\sum |a_m|^2/P)^{1/2}$. There are $I$ bins, and for large $q$,
$I C' (1 + \log qT)^{10} \le q^{1/100000}$, so the total is at most
$q^{L - 1/25000 + \eta + 1/100000}(\sum |a_m|^2/P)^{1/2}$. Since $\eta \le 10^{-5}$,
$L - 1/25000 + \eta + 1/100000 \le L - 1/50000$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Where the window comes from}\label{sec:ends}

Neglect the margin $1/25000$ and the allowance $\varpi$, and take a piece $g(\beta) = A(1 - \beta)$ of a density
curve. Then $E_1 \le L$ says that
\[
  \Bigl(L - \mu - \frac A2\Bigr)(1 - \beta) \ge \frac{1 - \mu}2 \quad \text{for } \mu \le 1, \qquad
  \mu \le L - \frac A2 \quad \text{for } \mu \ge 1.
\]
At $\mu = 1$ both read $L \ge 1 + A/2$, whatever $\beta$. So, neglecting the margins, orthogonality gives a window
around $P = q$ exactly when $L > 1 + A/2$ for the largest exponent $A$ of the curve, and the window shrinks to the
point $\mu = 1$ at $L = 1 + A/2$. The halving of $A$ comes from Cauchy--Schwarz over a bin, which takes the bin's size
to the power $1/2$.

The exponents of Section~\ref{sec:inputs} are largest at $\sigma = 5/7$, where Ingham's $3/(2 - \sigma)$ and Chen,
Gupta and Li's $4/(1 + \sigma)$ are both $7/3$, and in this approximation both ends of the window are set there. With
$\beta = 5/7$ and $A = 7/3$ the two conditions give
\[
  u_1 \ge \frac{35}{9L} - \frac43, \qquad u_2 \le 1 - \frac7{6L},
\]
which are $0.41527$ and $0.47542$ at $L = 278/125$. They meet at $L = 13/6$, below which these exponents leave no
window at all, whatever the certificate. The Hal\'asz--Montgomery inequality does not help at $\beta = 5/7$, where the
bins are large: there $E_3 \le L$ needs $L \ge 7/3 + \mu/8$, even with $\mathcal H$ as small as $P^{1/2}$. At
$\sigma = 7/9$, where $4/(1 + \sigma)$ meets Huxley's $3/(3\sigma - 1)$ at $A = 9/4$, the first condition gives
$u_1 \ge 27/(10L) - 4/5$, which is the weaker of the two below $L = 107/48 \approx 2.229$. For the classical exponents,
whose largest is $12/5$, at $\sigma = 3/4$, the threshold is $1 + 6/5 = 11/5$ (\S S6).

\subsection{The window at $278/125$}\label{sec:window-278}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:window}
The window $[u_1 L, u_2 L]$, with $u_1 = 41556/100000$ and $u_2 = 47531/100000$, is admissible for $g^*$ at
$L = 278/125$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On each piece of $g^*$, $g^*(\beta) = A(1 - \beta)$ with $A$ constant, or $g^* = 1$, so each $E_i$ is affine in
$\beta$ on the piece. Each $E_i$ increases with $\varpi$, since $L - \mu > 0$. As a function of $\mu$, $E_1$ is
convex: it decreases for $\mu < 1$, with slope $1/2 - \beta - \varpi \le 0$, and increases for $\mu > 1$, with slope
$1 - \beta - \varpi > 0$. On the top piece $[5/6, 11/12]$, $E_2$ increases with $\mu$, so for $\mu \le 1$ it is at most
$E_2(\beta, 1, \varpi) = E_1(\beta, 1, \varpi) \le E_1(\beta, \mu_2, \varpi)$, and $E_3$ decreases with $\mu$, with
slope $13/24 - \delta'/2 - \beta - \varpi < 0$. As a function of $\beta$ on a piece $A(1 - \beta)$, $E_1$ has slope
$L - \mu - A/2$, which is positive for $\mu \le \mu_2$ since every exponent of $g^*$ is at most
$2.3334 < 2(L - \mu_2)$ (on $g^* = 1$ the slope is $L - \mu > 0$), and on the top piece $E_3$ has slope
$L - \mu - A < 0$. Since $\mu_1 < 1 < \mu_2$, it therefore suffices that $E_1 \le L - 1/25000$ at the right end
$\beta_+$ of every piece below $5/6$ for $\mu \in \{\mu_1, \mu_2\}$, and at $(11/12, \mu_2)$ on the top piece, and that
$E_3 \le L - 1/25000$ at $(5/6, \mu_1)$, all at $\varpi = 4 \cdot 10^{-6}$. These are $134$ inequalities between
rational numbers, and each holds, the least with a slack of $5.3 \cdot 10^{-6}$. They hold also with the largest
exponents that Input~\ref{inp:density} allows, $A_k = 4/(1 + \sigma_k) + 10^{-4}$, the least then with a slack of
$5.6 \cdot 10^{-7}$, so the proposition holds for every choice of the $A_k$ that the input allows. Solved for $\mu$,
with $\Theta = L - 1/25000$, they say that $\mu_1$ is at least
$\bigl(L(\beta_+ + \varpi) + g(\beta_+)/2 + 1/2 - \Theta\bigr)/(\beta_+ + \varpi - 1/2)$ at the right end $\beta_+$
of every piece below $5/6$, and $\mu_2$ at most
$\bigl(\Theta - L(\beta_+ + \varpi) - g(\beta_+)/2\bigr)/(1 - \beta_+ - \varpi)$ at these ends and at $11/12$, with
$g(\beta_+)$ the piece's value there, besides the top piece's condition on $E_3$. Both bounds are set at
$\beta_+ = 1201/1680$, the right end of the first step of $[5/7, 131/168]$, and give the exact window
$[0.41554887\ldots, 0.47533423\ldots] \cdot L$, which contains the proposition's. At $\varpi = 0$, with no margin and
$\beta_+ = 5/7$, $A = 7/3$, they reduce to the closed forms of Section~\ref{sec:ends}. The $134$ inequalities are
checked in exact rational arithmetic (\S S5).
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:window}.
\end{proof}

The Hal\'asz--Montgomery branch is used only on the top piece of the curve, for $\mu \le 1$, where the bins are small,
and it is needed there: at $(11/12, \mu_1)$, $E_1$ exceeds $L - 1/25000$ by $1.4 \cdot 10^{-2}$, and orthogonality alone
would need $u_1 \ge 0.4304$. The kernel bound enters only through the corner of $E_3$ at $(5/6, \mu_1)$, which holds
with a slack of $0.094$. So the corners hold with any constant kernel exponent up to $0.684$ in place of $b(\mu)$, and
the Pólya--Vinogradov bound $\mathcal H \ll_\varepsilon q^{1/2 + \varepsilon}$ of Section~\ref{sec:two-bounds} would
do: the window does not need Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} for the kernel bound.

No bound for the kernels could move the ends. At the upper end $\mu_2 > 1$, and there the Hal\'asz--Montgomery
inequality never helps. At the lower end the binding corner is at $\beta = 1201/1680$, where
$g^*(\beta) + b(\mu_1) = 1.161 > 1$, so that $E_3 > E_1$ and the inequality loses to orthogonality. Every admissible
$\mathcal H$ is at least about $P^{1/2}$, since the mean square of $|k_{2P}(\psi)|$ over the non-trivial characters
mod $q$ is of order $P\varphi(q)/q$ when $P \le q^{1 - \varepsilon}$, and $g^*(\beta) + \mu_1/2 = 1.127 > 1$ still.
So the ends are set by orthogonality, that is, by the density exponent near $5/7$ and the large sieve.

\section{Type I estimates and Harman's sieve}\label{sec:type1}

Let $2 < L \le 5/2$ and $0 < u_1 < u_2 < 1$; this section uses nothing else of $L$ and the window, and
Section~\ref{sec:certificate} adds the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:route}. Below,
`the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2}' means the Type~II estimate of that theorem at this $L$ on the window
$[u_1 L, u_2 L]$, which Proposition~\ref{prop:type2-gen} gives on every admissible window; Theorem~\ref{thm:type2} is its
case $L = 278/125$, $u_1 = 41556/100000$, $u_2 = 47531/100000$. Let $x = q^L$ and let $a$ be prime to $q$. The
\emph{window's range} is $[x^{u_1}, x^{u_2}]$, the range of the scale $P$ of the Type~II variable, which runs over
$(P, 2P]$, and its \emph{reflection} is $[x^{1 - u_2}, x^{1 - u_1}]$. The sets of the sieve are
\[
  \mathcal A = \{n \in (x, 2x] : n \equiv a \ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)\}, \qquad
  \mathcal B = \{n \in (x, 2x] : (n, q) = 1\},
\]
whose integers are those of $(N_1, N_2]$ with $N_1 = \lfloor x \rfloor$ and $N_2 = \lfloor 2x \rfloor$.
For a set $\mathcal C$ of integers, an integer $r$ and $z \ge 1$, $\mathcal C_r$ is the set of the multiples of $r$
in $\mathcal C$, and $S(\mathcal C_r, z)$ the number of $n \in \mathcal C_r$ with no prime factor of $n/r$ below $z$.
When $r$ has no prime factor below $z$, as in every use below, this is the number of multiples of $r$ in $\mathcal C$
with no prime factor below $z$.
With $z_{\mathrm{top}} = \lfloor N_2^{1/2} \rfloor + 1$, an integer of $(N_1, N_2]$ with no prime factor below
$z_{\mathrm{top}}$ is prime, so $S(\mathcal A, z_{\mathrm{top}})$ is the number of primes in $\mathcal A$. The sieve
compares $\mathcal A$ with $\mathcal B/\varphi(q)$ through the weight
\[
  \mathbf 1[n \equiv a] - \frac{\mathbf 1[(n, q) = 1]}{\varphi(q)}
  = \frac1{\varphi(q)} \sum_{\chi \in \mathcal X^*} \overline\chi(a) \chi(n),
\]
which is orthogonality: the trivial character cancels, and the other characters carry the difference. This section
states the tools of Harman's sieve \cite{harman83, harman} in the form the proof uses. None of them is new:
Lemmas~\ref{lem:type1} to~\ref{lem:chains} use standard arguments, among them a Fourier separation of variables, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:harman} is Harman's comparison. The proofs of Lemma~\ref{lem:family} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:harman} are in the supplement (\S S2 and \S S3).

\subsection{Type~I}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:type1}
Let $0 \le N_1 \le N_2$ be integers and let $M > 0$ be prime to $q$. The number of $n \in (N_1, N_2]$ with $n \equiv a \pmod q$ and
$M \mid n$ is $(N_2 - N_1)/(Mq)$ within $1$, and the number of $n \in (N_1, N_2]$ prime to $q$ with $M \mid n$ is
$\varphi(q)(N_2 - N_1)/(Mq)$ within $\varphi(q)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $(M, q) = 1$, the Chinese remainder theorem gives $c$ with $c \equiv 0 \pmod M$ and $c \equiv a \pmod q$, and
the two conditions $n \equiv a \pmod q$ and $M \mid n$ together say $n \equiv c \pmod{Mq}$. For $r \ge 1$ the number of
$n \in (N_1, N_2]$ with $n \equiv c \pmod r$ is $\lfloor (N_2 - c)/r \rfloor - \lfloor (N_1 - c)/r \rfloor$, which
differs from $(N_2 - N_1)/r$ by less than $1$. With $r = Mq$ this is the first count. The integers prime to $q$ form the
$\varphi(q)$ residue classes $b \bmod q$ with $(b, q) = 1$, and adding the first count over these classes gives the
second.
\end{proof}

So $|\mathcal A_M| - |\mathcal B_M|/\varphi(q)$ is at most $2$ in absolute value for every $M$ prime to $q$. This is
all the Type~I information the sieve needs. It is used at the leaves of Proposition~\ref{prop:harman}, where what
matters is that the number of counts, at most a constant times $q^{L - 1 - 2c}$ with $c = (L - 1 - b_0)/4 > 0$
(\S S3), keeps
their errors small against the size $x/q$ of the main terms; the size of each modulus does not.

\subsection{Type~II}

Theorem~\ref{thm:type2} bounds Dirichlet polynomials. The sieve needs sums over $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal B$ of
integers $n = m p_1 \cdots p_K$ whose primes are cut out by comparisons. Fix a smooth function $f_0 \ge 0$ supported
in $[1/2, 2]$, integers $K_0, J_0 \ge 0$ and reals $e_1 > 0$ and $E_0, \Lambda_0, A_0 \ge 0$. A \emph{family} consists of
\begin{itemize}
\item an integer scale $P$ with $x^{u_1} \le P \le x^{u_2}$, a set $S \subseteq (P, 2P]$ and coefficients $|a_m| \le A_0$;
\item between $1$ and $K_0$ primes $p_i \le 2Q_i$ with weights $f_0(p_i/Q_i)$, at scales $Q_i \ge q^{e_1}$ with
  $Q_1 \cdots Q_K \le 2^{K_0} x/P$;
\item at most $J_0$ comparisons, conditions on $(m, p)$ that hold when $\ell_j(m, p) > 0$ and fail when
  $\ell_j(m, p) < 0$, where $\ell_j(m, p) = \lambda_j(m) + \sum_i e_{ji} \log p_i$ with $|e_{ji}| \le E_0$ and
  $\ell_j \le \Lambda_0 \log q$ for every $m \in S$ and all primes $p_i \le 2Q_i$, each comparison with a
  \emph{pivot}, a prime $p_i$ with $e_{ji} = \pm 1$.
\end{itemize}
Its sum over $\mathcal A - \mathcal B/\varphi(q)$ is the sum of $a_m \prod_i f_0(p_i/Q_i)$ times the weight above at
$n = m p_1 \cdots p_K$, over the $(m, p)$ that satisfy the comparisons. The integers $n$ need not lie in $(x, 2x]$; where
they must, two comparisons say so.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:family}
Assume the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2}. Given $f_0$, $K_0$, $e_1$, $J_0$, $E_0$, $\Lambda_0$ and $A_0$, there
are $\delta > 0$ and $q_0$ such that for every $q \ge q_0$, every $a$ prime to $q$ and every family, its sum over
$\mathcal A - \mathcal B/\varphi(q)$ is at most $A_0 q^{L - \delta}/\varphi(q)$ in absolute value.
\end{lemma}

The proof is in \S S2. By orthogonality the family's sum is a sum over the non-trivial characters.
Replacing each comparison by a smooth cut-off of its form makes the family smooth; Fourier inversion then separates the
variables, and Theorem~\ref{thm:type2} bounds the smooth family at each frequency up to a small power of $q$; the decay of
the cut-offs' Fourier transforms handles the rest. The difference between the sharp
and the smooth comparisons lies near the boundaries $\ell_j = 0$; the proof bounds it by families of the same kind,
again through Theorem~\ref{thm:type2}, and by a count along the pivot.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:family-sharp}
Assume the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2}. Given $K_0$, $J_0$, $e_1 > 0$, $E_0 \ge 0$ and $\Lambda_1 \ge 0$, there
are $\delta > 0$ and $q_0$ such that the following holds for every $q \ge q_0$ and every $a$ prime to $q$. Let $m$ run
over a set $S \subseteq (P_1, P_2]$ of integers, for integers $P_1$ and $P_2$ with $q^{u_1 L} \le P_1$ and
$P_2 \le q^{u_2 L}$, with coefficients
$|a_m| \le 1$, and let $1 \le K \le K_0$ primes $p_1, \dots, p_K$ run over all the primes up to $2x$. Let $J \le J_0$
comparisons, with forms $\ell_j = \lambda_j(m) + \sum_i e_{ji} \log p_i$, $|e_{ji}| \le E_0$,
$\lambda_j(m) \le \Lambda_1 \log q$ for $m \in S$, and pivots, cut out the $(m, p)$, and suppose that wherever they all
hold, for $m \in S$ and any primes $p_i$, every $p_i \ge 2q^{e_1}$ and $m p_1 \cdots p_K \le 2x$. Then the sum of
$a_m(\mathbf 1[n \equiv a] - \mathbf 1[(n, q) = 1]/\varphi(q))$ at $n = m p_1 \cdots p_K$ over the $(m, p)$ that satisfy
the comparisons is at most $q^{L - \delta}/\varphi(q)$ in absolute value.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Split $(P_1, P_2]$ into the dyadic blocks $(2^b P_1, 2^{b + 1} P_1]$, and each prime variable by a smooth partition of
unity: take for the $f_0$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:family} the fixed function $f_0(y) = \psi(y) - \psi(y/2)$, with $\psi$
smooth and nondecreasing, $0$ on $(-\infty, 1/2]$ and $1$ on $[1, \infty)$. It is supported in $[1/2, 2]$, takes values
in $[0, 1]$, and $\sum_{t \ge 0} f_0(y/2^t) = 1$ for every $y \ge 1$; the primes are at most $2x < 2^M$,
$M = \lfloor \log_2 (2x) \rfloor + 1$, so only the scales $2^t$ with $t \le M$ occur. This writes the sum as a sum of
at most
$(3 + 4 \log q)^{K_0 + 1}$ pieces. In each, $m$ runs over a block, which lies in $(P, 2P]$ with $P$ in the window's
range, and the primes carry the weights $f_0(p_i/2^{t_i})$ at the scales $Q_i = 2^{t_i}$; on its box every form is at
most $(\Lambda_1 + K_0 E_0 (L + 3)) \log q$. A piece with some $2^{t_i} < q^{e_1}$ vanishes, since its weights force
$p_i < 2q^{e_1}$; and so does a piece with $\prod_i 2^{t_i} > 2^{K_0 + 1} x/P$, since its weights force
$m \prod_i p_i > 2x$. In a piece the primes may run over the range $p_i \le 2Q_i$ of a family instead of the primes up
to $2x$, since a term with some $p_i > 2x$ violates the comparisons, which force $m \prod_i p_i \le 2x$. Every other
piece is a family, with $K_0 + 1$ in place of $K_0$, $A_0 = 1$ and
$\Lambda_0 = \Lambda_1 + K_0 E_0 (L + 3)$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:family} bounds it by $q^{L - \delta_1}/\varphi(q)$. For
large $q$ the number of pieces is at most $q^{\delta_1/2}$, and $\delta = \delta_1/2$ will do.
\end{proof}

The sieve meets these sums with the rest of $n$ given only as a rough number. The next lemma writes such sums as
families. A \emph{comparison} of a tuple $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ of primes is a condition $c + \sum_i e_i \log r_i > 0$
(or $\ge 0$) with a constant term $c$ and real coefficients $e_i$, and a \emph{pivot} of it is an index $i$ with
$e_i = \pm 1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:chains} takes the coefficients in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:harman} in
$[-1, 1]$, with a pivot.

\begin{lemma}[chains of primes]\label{lem:chains}
Assume the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2}. Given $k \ge 1$, $J_0$, $e_0 > 0$ and $\Lambda_1 \ge 0$, there are
$\delta > 0$ and $q_0$ such that the following holds for every $q \ge q_0$ and every $a$ prime to $q$. Let
$\mathbf r = (r_1, \dots, r_k)$ run over the $k$-tuples of primes up to $N_2$ that satisfy at most $J_0$ comparisons
with coefficients in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and constant terms at most $\Lambda_1 \log q$, and suppose that every tuple of primes that satisfies the comparisons has
$r_i \ge q^{e_0}$ for every $i$, and that either every such tuple satisfies (a), or every such tuple satisfies (b):
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $r_k \le r_i$ for every $i$, and $W_0 < r_1 \cdots r_k \le W_1$, for integers $W_0$ and $W_1$ with $W_1 \le N_1$,
  $q^{u_1 L} \le \lfloor N_1/W_1 \rfloor$ and $\lfloor N_2/(W_0 + 1) \rfloor \le q^{u_2 L}$;
\item[(b)] $r_1 > \dots > r_k$, and $P_1 < \prod_{i \in T} r_i \le P_2$ for a fixed set $T$ of indices, for
  integers $P_1$ and $P_2$ with $q^{u_1 L} \le P_1$ and $P_2 \le q^{u_2 L}$.
\end{enumerate}
Then, with $r = r_1 \cdots r_k$,
\[
  \Bigl|\sum_{\mathbf r} \Bigl(S(\mathcal A_r, r_k) - \frac{S(\mathcal B_r, r_k)}{\varphi(q)}\Bigr)\Bigr|
  \le \frac{q^{L - \delta}}{\varphi(q)}.
\]
Under (b) the same bound holds for the exact products: for the sum over $\mathbf r$ with $N_1 < r \le N_2$ of
$\mathbf 1[r \equiv a] - \mathbf 1[(r, q) = 1]/\varphi(q)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
An integer $n$ counted for $\mathbf r$ is $n = rm \in (N_1, N_2]$ with $m$ free of prime factors below $r_k$; since every
$r_i \ge r_k$, this says that $n$ itself has no prime factor below $r_k$.

(a) Here $m$, the rest of $n$, is the Type~II variable. Since $W_0 < r \le W_1$, $m$ lies in
$S = (\lfloor N_1/W_1 \rfloor, \lfloor N_2/(W_0 + 1) \rfloor]$, inside the window's range. So the sum is one of the kind
of Lemma~\ref{lem:family-sharp}, with $a_m = 1$ on $S$ and the primes $r_1, \dots, r_k$. Its comparisons are the given
ones, each pivoted on a prime with a nonzero coefficient (one with none is a constant condition); the roughness of $m$,
$\log \mathrm{lpf}(m) - \log r_k \ge 0$, with $\mathrm{lpf}(m)$ the least prime factor of $m$ and the pivot $r_k$; and
$N_1 < mr \le N_2$, two comparisons with the forms $\log m + \sum_i \log r_i - \log N_1$ and its counterpart. Their
$m$-parts are at most $(L + 1) \log q$. Wherever they hold, every $r_i \ge q^{e_0} \ge 2q^{e_0/2}$ and $mr \le 2x$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:family-sharp} applies, with $K_0 = k + 1$, $J_0 + 3$ comparisons, $E_0 = 1$, $e_0/2$ in place of $e_1$
and $\Lambda_1 + L + 1$ in place of $\Lambda_1$.

(b) Here $m = \prod_{i \in T} r_i$ is the variable, and the rest of $n$ must be written in primes. The cofactor
$n/r$ has no prime factor below $r_k \ge q^{e_0}$ and is at most $2x \le q^{L + 1}$, so it is the product of a unique
non-increasing tuple $y_1 \ge \dots \ge y_s \ge r_k$ of primes with $s \le s_1 = \lceil (L + 1)/e_0 \rceil$. For each
$s$, regroup the pairs $(\mathbf r, y)$ by $m$: since $\mathbf r$ is strictly decreasing, $m$ is a squarefree integer
of $(P_1, P_2]$ with $|T|$ prime factors, which, read in decreasing order, are the $r_i$ with $i \in T$, so $m$
determines them. The other variables are the primes $r_i$ with $i \notin T$ and $y_1, \dots, y_s$. The conditions are
the given comparisons (which imply $r_1 > \dots > r_k$), $y_b \le y_a$ for $a \le b$, $y_a \ge r_k$ for every $a$, and
$N_1 < m \prod_{i \notin T} r_i \prod_l y_l \le N_2$, each a comparison whose $m$-part reads the $r_i$ with $i \in T$
off $m$. Those that involve only these $r_i$ are conditions on $m$, and give coefficients $a_m \in \{0, 1\}$ on
$(P_1, P_2]$; each of the others has a nonzero coefficient at a variable outside $T$, its pivot. There are at most
$J_0 + s_1^2 + s_1 + 2$ of them, with $m$-parts at most $(\Lambda_1 + 2L + 2) \log q$, and wherever they hold every
prime is at least $q^{e_0}$ and the product is at most $2x$. If $T = \{1, \dots, k\}$ and $s = 0$, no prime is left:
then $n = m \le P_2 < N_1$ for large $q$, and that sum is empty. Lemma~\ref{lem:family-sharp}, with
$K_0 = s_1 + k + 1$, $J_0 + s_1^2 + s_1 + 2$ comparisons, $E_0 = 1$, $e_0/2$ in place of $e_1$ and
$\Lambda_1 + 2L + 2$ in place of $\Lambda_1$, bounds each of the other sums by $q^{L - \delta_1}/\varphi(q)$; there
are at most $s_1 + 1$ of them, and $\delta = \delta_1/2$ will do for large $q$. The exact products are the case $s = 0$
alone, with no cofactor: the same regrouping makes their sum one sum of the kind of Lemma~\ref{lem:family-sharp}.
\end{proof}

In (a) the product $r$ lies in the reflection of the window, and in (b) a product of some of the primes lies in the
window. If $W_0 + 1 \ge x^{1 - u_2'}$ (for instance $W_0 = \lfloor x^{1 - u_2'} \rfloor$) and
$W_1 = \lfloor x^{1 - u_1'} \rfloor$ with $u_1 < u_1' < u_2' < u_2$, the
conditions of (a) hold for large $q$, since $\lfloor N_1/W_1 \rfloor \ge x^{u_1'} - 2 \ge x^{u_1}$ and
$\lfloor N_2/(W_0 + 1) \rfloor \le 2x^{u_2'} \le x^{u_2}$. Section~\ref{sec:certificate} uses (a) in this way, with
$u_1' = U_1/D$ and $u_2' = U_2/D$: the margins absorb the factor $2$ of the upper end $2x$ and the rounding of
$N_1/W_1$.

\subsection{Harman's comparison}

\begin{proposition}[Harman's comparison, in the form used]\label{prop:harman}
Assume the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2}. Fix $j \ge 0$, $J_0$, $\Lambda_1 \ge 0$, $\varepsilon_H > 0$, and exponents
$a_0 > 0$ and $b_0$ with $a_0 + b_0 < (1 - u_1)L$ and $(1 - u_2)L < b_0 < L - 1$. There are $\delta > 0$ and $q_0$ such
that the following holds for every $q \ge q_0$ and every $a$ prime to $q$. Let $(r_1, \dots, r_j)$ run over the ordered
$j$-tuples of primes up to $2x$ that satisfy at most $J_0$ comparisons, with coefficients in $[-1, 1]$, pivots and
constant terms at most $\Lambda_1 \log q$, which force every $r_i \ge z_0 = \lceil q^{a_0} \rceil$ and
$r = r_1 \cdots r_j \le q^{b_0}$. Then
\[
  \Bigl|\sum_{(r_1, \dots, r_j)} \Bigl(S(\mathcal A_r, z_0) - \frac{S(\mathcal B_r, z_0)}{\varphi(q)}\Bigr)\Bigr|
  \le \frac{\varepsilon_H\, x}{\varphi(q) \log q} + \frac{q^{L - \delta}}{\varphi(q)}.
\]
For $j = 0$ the sum is the single term $S(\mathcal A, z_0) - S(\mathcal B, z_0)/\varphi(q)$.
\end{proposition}

The proof (\S S3) iterates Buchstab's identity \cite{buchstab} from $z_0$ down to a small
power $w$ of $q$, stopping a branch as soon as its product exceeds $X' = \lfloor q^{b_0} \rfloor$. A stopped branch has
its product in $(X', z_0 X']$, the reflection of the window, and its cofactor in the window's range, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:family-sharp} evaluates the stops. The leaves are sifted only by the primes below $w$, and Brun's sieve
\cite{brun}, in the form of the fundamental lemma of sieve theory \cite{friedlander-iwaniec, iwaniec-kowalski},
compares their $\mathcal A$- and $\mathcal B$-counts, with an error that the elementary bound
$\prod_{p < w}(1 - 1/p) \le 1/\log w$, Mertens' theorem for the sum over the leaves and the choice of the sieve's
levels make at most $\varepsilon_H x/(\varphi(q) \log q)$.

\section{The decomposition and its certificate}\label{sec:certificate}

This section bounds $S(\mathcal A, z_{\mathrm{top}})$ from below. The decomposition writes it by Buchstab's identity as
a signed sum of sifted terms. Each term is evaluated, that is compared with its $\mathcal B$-version by
Lemma~\ref{lem:chains} or Proposition~\ref{prop:harman}, or, if it has sign $+$, dropped, which costs its
$\mathcal B$-version. The certificate is an upper bound for the total cost, computed exactly in integers.

\subsection{Exponents and the grid}

The exponent of a prime $p$ is $\alpha = \log p/\log x$. Fix a denominator $D = 2^{40}$ and integers $U_1 < U_2$ with
$u_1 D < U_1$ and $U_2 < u_2 D$, so that $u_1 < U_1/D < U_2/D < u_2$; the grid of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} rounds the
window's ends inward, $U_1 = \lceil u_1 D \rceil$ and $U_2 = \lfloor u_2 D \rfloor$, which qualify since $u_1 D$ and
$u_2 D$ are not integers. Let $v = (U_2 - U_1)/D$. The decomposition sifts the primes of exponent at least $v$. The
grid has $N$ cells a side, of side $h = \lceil (D/2 - (U_2 - U_1))/N \rceil / D$; the cell $k$ is the range
$[e_k, e_k + h)$ of exponents with $e_k = v + kh$, for $0 \le k < N$, so that the cells cover $[v, v + Nh)$, and
$v + Nh \ge 1/2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:route} asks $v + Nh > 1/2$, which holds on the grid of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}).
Here $N = 340$. The primes of the two deepest levels of the decomposition, $p_5$ and $p_6$, have their
exponents in the cells of a \emph{coarse grid} with the same window and $N_C = N/4 = 85$ cells a side, each four
consecutive cells of the grid: its side is $h_C = 4h$, and its cell $b$ is $[e^C_b, e^C_b + h_C)$ with
$e^C_b = v + b h_C = e_{4b}$.

The set of \emph{evaluable} exponents is
\[
  \mathcal U = (U_1/D,\ U_2/D) \cup (1 - U_2/D,\ 1 - U_1/D).
\]
A box, a product of cells, is \emph{surely evaluable} if for some subset of its coordinates the sum of the exponents
lies in $\mathcal U$ throughout the box. A box is tested exactly: the subset sums of a box range over intervals with
rational ends.

\subsection{The decomposition}

\emph{The parameters.} Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:harman} with $a_0 = vL$ and $b_0 = (1 - U_2/D)L$, so that
$z_0 = \lceil x^v \rceil$ and $X' = \lfloor x^{1 - U_2/D} \rfloor$. Its hypotheses hold:
$a_0 + b_0 = (1 - U_1/D)L < (1 - u_1)L$, since $U_1/D > u_1$; $b_0 > (1 - u_2)L$, since $U_2/D < u_2$; and
$b_0 < L - 1$ is the condition $L U_2 > D$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:route}. For large $x$, $z_{\mathrm{top}} \le X'$, since
$2U_2 < D$, and $z_0 X' \le x^{1 - U_1/D}$ up to rounding: $v$ is the largest floor for which the stops of
Proposition~\ref{prop:harman} stay in the reflection of the rounded window $[U_1/D, U_2/D]$.

\emph{The tree.} Buchstab's identity
\[
  S(\mathcal A_m, z) = S(\mathcal A_m, z_0) - \sum_{z_0 \le p < z} S(\mathcal A_{mp}, p),
\]
applied to $S(\mathcal A, z_{\mathrm{top}})$ and repeated, introduces primes $p_1 > p_2 > \cdots$, each at least
$z_0$, with exponents $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots$. A \emph{term of depth $k$} is a sum of $S(\mathcal A_{p_1 \cdots p_k}, p_k)$
over the primes whose exponents lie in a box of cells, fine for $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_4$ and coarse for $\alpha_5$ and
$\alpha_6$; its sign is $(-1)^k$. Each use of the identity on a term with $p_1 \cdots p_k \le X'$ also gives the term
$S(\mathcal A_{p_1 \cdots p_k}, z_0)$, which Proposition~\ref{prop:harman} evaluates, with the cells, the order of the
primes and the earlier splits as its comparisons. A term of depth $k$ is treated by the first of the following rules
that applies.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(E)] If its box is surely evaluable, it is evaluated by Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}.
\item[(O)] If its box lies in $\alpha_k \ge \alpha_{k - 1}$, that is, the cell of $\alpha_k$ starts at or above the end of
  the cell of $\alpha_{k - 1}$, or if the lower corner $(e_{c_1}, \dots, e_{c_k})$ of its box, with $c_i$ the cell of
  $\alpha_i$, has $e_{c_1} + \cdots + e_{c_{k - 1}} + 2e_{c_k} > 1$, so that $\alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_{k - 1} + 2\alpha_k \ge 1 + 1/D$ on the box, it is empty for $k \ge 3$ (below), and
  for $k = 2$ it is empty if its box lies in $\alpha_2 \ge \alpha_1$ and otherwise counts only products $p_1 p_2$; these
  non-empty cells of depth two, with $e_1 + 2e_2 > 1$, are the \emph{corner} cells, and they are dropped, at a
  negligible cost (Lemma~\ref{lem:drop}).
\item[(S)] Otherwise, a term with sign $-$ is split at $X'$: the part with $p_1 \cdots p_k > X'$ is evaluated by
  Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}(a), with the cofactor as the Type~II variable, and the part with $p_1 \cdots p_k \le X'$ is
  decomposed by Buchstab's identity, which gives $S(\mathcal A_{p_1 \cdots p_k}, z_0)$ and terms of depth $k + 1$.
\item[(D)] A term with sign $+$ is dropped, or, if the sharp rule below allows it and the certificate chooses it,
  split and decomposed as in (S).
\end{enumerate}
For $k \ge 3$ the order of (E) and (O) does not matter, since the terms of (O) are empty.
At depth one there is no split: a term whose cell lies inside the window is evaluated by Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}(b) with
$T = \{1\}$, and every other one is decomposed, since $p_1 < z_{\mathrm{top}} \le X'$. The terms of depth six are
evaluated or dropped, and not decomposed. At depth two a cell to which (E) and (O) do not apply is a
\emph{candidate}; at depths four and six such a cell is \emph{counted}. Table~\ref{tab:tree} sums up, and
Figure~\ref{fig:cells} shows the cells of depth two.

\emph{Empty terms.} A term of depth $k \ge 3$ lies below a split, so $p_1 \cdots p_{k - 1} \le X'$ on it, and
$p_k < p_{k - 1} < X'^{1/2}$, since $p_{k-1}^2 < p_1 p_2 \le X'$. If its box has $\alpha_k \ge \alpha_{k - 1}$, it is empty.
If the box has $\alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_{k - 1} + 2\alpha_k \ge 1 + 1/D$, then for large $x$,
$p_1 \cdots p_k^2 \ge x^{1 + 1/D} > 2x$, so a cofactor $m$ with no prime factor below $p_k$ is $1$, and
$n = p_1 \cdots p_k < X'^{3/2} < N_1$, since $\tfrac32 (1 - U_2/D) < \tfrac32 (1 - 1/L) \le 9/10$ by $L U_2 > D$ and
$L \le 5/2$; so the term counts no integer of $(N_1, N_2]$. At depth two the same holds for
$\alpha_2 \ge \alpha_1$, while outside $\alpha_1 + 2\alpha_2 \le 1$ the cofactor is $1$ and the term counts the
products $n = p_1 p_2 \in (x, 2x]$.

\emph{The sharp rule.} It allows a candidate cell $(i, j)$ to be decomposed only if the upper corner of its box has
$(e_i + h) + (e_j + h) < 1 - U_1/D$, and the upper corner of each of its cells $(i, j, k)$ of depth three to which (E)
and (O) do not apply has $(e_i + h) + (e_j + h) + (e_k + h) < 1 - U_1/D$; and a counted cell of depth four only if the
same tests hold for its box and for the boxes of its cells of depth five to which (E) and (O) do not apply, whose last
cell is coarse, with upper end $e^C_b + h_C$. Then the cell, and every term below it that is split at $X'$, has
$\alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_k < 1 - U_1/D$ on its box, so that its part above $X'$ has
$X' < p_1 \cdots p_k < x^{1 - U_1/D}$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}(a) applies there, with $W_0 = X'$ and
$W_1 = \lfloor x^{1 - U_1/D} \rfloor$: the cofactor lies in $(\lfloor N_1/W_1 \rfloor, \lfloor N_2/(X' + 1) \rfloor]$,
which is inside the window's range for large $q$ by the remark after Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}.

\emph{Evaluation.} Let the box of a term of depth $k$ be surely evaluable, with $\sum_{i \in T} \alpha_i \in \mathcal U$
on it. If the sum lies in $(U_1/D, U_2/D)$, Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}(b) applies with this $T$, the cells, the order of
the primes and the earlier splits as comparisons. If it lies in $(1 - U_2/D, 1 - U_1/D)$, then $T$ is the set of all
the primes, since a proper subset has product at most $p_1 \cdots p_{k - 1} \le X' \le x^{1 - U_2/D}$; so the cofactor
is the variable, and Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}(a) applies.

\subsection{The cost of a dropped term}

The cost of a dropped term is the number of its integers in $\mathcal B$, and their cofactors are rough numbers. Let
$\Phi(y, z)$ be the number of integers in $(y, 2y]$ with no prime factor below $z$, and let
\[
  \omega^*(u) = \begin{cases}
    0, & u < 1, \\
    1/u, & 1 \le u < 2, \\
    (1 + \log(u - 1))/u, & 2 \le u \le 3, \\
    0.565073, & u > 3.
  \end{cases}
\]
On $[1, 3]$ this is Buchstab's function $\omega$, whose maximum on $[2, 3]$ is $0.567143\ldots$, the root $t$ of
$t e^t = 1$, at $u = 1 + 1/t$, where $(u - 1)\log(u - 1) = 1$ \cite{cheer-goldston}; above $3$ it is the constant
that the induction of the next lemma needs.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rough}
For every $U$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, for all large integers $z$ and all $y \ge 1$ with $u = \log 2y/\log z \le U$,
$\Phi(y, z) \le (\omega^*(u) + \varepsilon)\, y/\log z$.
\end{lemma}

This is an upper bound of the kind that Buchstab's asymptotic for rough numbers gives \cite{buchstab, tenenbaum}, with
an explicit constant above $3$. The proof (\S S4) is an induction on $u$ with Buchstab's inequality, the prime number
theorem and a delay inequality, $1 + M(u) \le u \omega^*(u)$ for $u \ge 2$, where $M(u)$ is $\int_1^{u - 1} \omega^*$
with $\omega^*$ replaced by $0.5671433 \ge 0.567143\ldots$ on $[2, 3]$. Above $3$ the inequality holds exactly when
$1 + \log 2 + 0.5671433 \le 4c_*$ for the constant $c_*$ of $\omega^*$ there, that is when $c_* \ge 0.5650726\ldots$,
and $0.565073$ is that bound rounded up.

A dropped term of depth two over the cell $(i, j)$ counts the $n = p_1 p_2 m \in (x, 2x]$ whose cofactor $m$ has no
prime factor below $p_2$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:rough} and the prime number theorem its $\mathcal B$-version is, for large
$q$, at most $x/\log x$ times
\[
  \iint_{\text{cell}} \omega^*\Bigl(\frac{1 - \alpha_1 - \alpha_2}{\alpha_2}\Bigr)
  \frac{d\alpha_1\, d\alpha_2}{\alpha_1 \alpha_2^2},
\]
plus $\varepsilon$, and similarly at depth four with
$d\alpha_1 \cdots d\alpha_4/(\alpha_1 \alpha_2 \alpha_3 \alpha_4^2)$ and the argument
$(1 - \alpha_1 - \cdots - \alpha_4)/\alpha_4$, and at depth six with
$d\alpha_1 \cdots d\alpha_6/(\alpha_1 \cdots \alpha_5 \alpha_6^2)$ and the argument
$(1 - \alpha_1 - \cdots - \alpha_6)/\alpha_6$. The certificate bounds each integral by a bound $\Omega$ for $\omega^*$
on the cell's range of arguments times the integral of the rest, a product of integrals of $d\alpha/\alpha$ and one
of $d\alpha/\alpha^2$ over the cells. It bounds the first kind from above by the trapezoid rule, since $1/\alpha$ is
convex, and takes the second exactly:
\begin{gather*}
  \int_e^{e + h} \frac{d\alpha}\alpha = \log\Bigl(1 + \frac he\Bigr) \le t(e, h) = \frac{h(2e + h)}{2e(e + h)}, \\
  \int_e^{e + h} \frac{d\alpha}{\alpha^2} = s(e, h) = \frac h{e(e + h)}.
\end{gather*}
It works in units of $1/\mathcal K$ with $\mathcal K = 2^{60}$, every quotient rounded up, except the constants $\kappa$
of the kept parts below, which are rounded down. The bound $\Omega$ for
$\omega^*$ on a cell of depth two, whose arguments lie in $[u_{\min}, u_{\max})$ with
$u_{\min} = (1 - e_i - e_j - 2h)/(e_j + h)$ and $u_{\max} = (1 + 1/D - e_i - e_j)/e_j$, is found by cases, since
$\omega^*$ is not monotone:
\begin{itemize}
\item $0$ if $u_{\max} \le 1$;
\item $0.565073$, rounded up in units of $1/\mathcal K$, if $u_{\min} \ge 3$;
\item $1/\max(u_{\min}, 1)$, rounded up, if $u_{\max} \le 2$;
\item otherwise the largest of $1/\max(u_{\min}, 1)$, if $u_{\min} < 2$; of a bound for $\omega^*$ on the part of the
  range in $[2, 3]$; and of $0.565073$, if $u_{\max} > 3$.
\end{itemize}
On $[2, 3]$, $\omega^* = \omega$ increases up to $2.76$ and decreases from $2.77$ on (\S S4), so the
bound there is $\omega(u_{\max})$ if $u_{\max} \le 2.76$, $\omega(u_{\min})$ if $u_{\min} \ge 2.77$, and $0.5671433$, at
least its maximum $0.567143\ldots$ there, otherwise. At a rational $w \in [2, 3]$ the certificate bounds $\omega(w) = (1 + \log(w - 1))/w$ by
$\log(w - 1) \le \ell_k + (y - 1/y)/2$, where $r_k = 1 + k/16 \le w - 1 < r_k + 1/16$, $y = (w - 1)/r_k \ge 1$, and
$\ell_k \ge \log r_k$ is one of seventeen rationals of a table, each checked against the exponential's series; the
second term is $\log y \le (y - 1/y)/2$, the trapezoid rule again, which here errs by less than $4 \cdot 10^{-5}$. At
depths four and six $\Omega$ is found in the same way: for a box of cells $c_1, \dots, c_k$, with lower ends $e_{c_i}$
and widths $w_i$ ($h$, or $h_C$ for a coarse cell), the arguments lie in $[u_{\min}, u_{\max})$ with
\[
  u_{\min} = \frac{1 - E - (w_1 + \cdots + w_k)}{e_{c_k} + w_k}, \qquad u_{\max} = \frac{1 + 1/D - E}{e_{c_k}},
\]
where $E = e_{c_1} + \cdots + e_{c_k}$; at depth two these are the ends above. The \emph{bound} of a cell is then
\begin{gather*}
  \Delta_2(i, j) = \bigl\lceil \mathcal K \Omega\, t(e_i, h)\, s(e_j, h) \bigr\rceil, \\
  \Delta_4(i, j, k, l) = \bigl\lceil \mathcal K \Omega\, t(e_i, h)\, t(e_j, h)\, t(e_k, h)\, s(e_l, h) \bigr\rceil, \\
  \Delta_6(i, j, k, l, b, c) = \bigl\lceil \mathcal K \Omega\, t(e_i, h)\, t(e_j, h)\, t(e_k, h)\, t(e_l, h)\,
    t(e^C_b, h_C)\, s(e^C_c, h_C) \bigr\rceil,
\end{gather*}
with $\mathcal K \Omega$ the rounded value; at depth six $b$ and $c$ are coarse cells.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:drop}
For every $\varepsilon_c > 0$ and all large $q$, with $X = x/\log x$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the $\mathcal B$-version of the dropped term of a candidate cell $(i, j)$ is at most
  $(\Delta_2(i, j)/\mathcal K + \varepsilon_c) X$;
\item that of a dropped counted cell of depth four is at most $(\Delta_4/\mathcal K + \varepsilon_c) X$, and that of a
  dropped counted cell of depth six at most $(\Delta_6/\mathcal K + \varepsilon_c) X$;
\item the corner cells together contribute $o(X)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For a cell $k$, write $\mathcal P_k$ for the set of the primes $p$ with $x^{e_k} \le p < x^{e_k + h}$.

(1) The $\mathcal B$-version of the term counts the $n \in (x, 2x]$ prime to $q$ of the form $n = p_1 p_2 m$ with
$(p_1, p_2)$ in the cell and $m$ without prime factors below $p_2$. Dropping the conditions $(n, q) = 1$, $p_2 < p_1$,
$z_0 \le p_2$ and $p_1 < z_{\mathrm{top}}$, and mapping $n$ to $m$, it is at most
\[
  \sum_{p_1 \in \mathcal P_i} \sum_{p_2 \in \mathcal P_j} \Phi\Bigl(\frac{x}{p_1 p_2}, p_2\Bigr).
\]
A candidate has $e_i + 2e_j \le 1$, and $e_j \ge v \ge 4h$, so $e_i + e_j + 2h < 1$ and $y = x/(p_1 p_2) \ge 1$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:rough}, with $U = 2/e_j$, applies to $\Phi(y, p_2)$, with
$u = \log 2y/\log p_2 = (1 - \alpha_1 - \alpha_2 + \tau)/\alpha_2$ and $\tau = \log 2/\log x$. Since $\tau < 1/D$ once
$\log x > D \log 2$, $u < u_{\max}$, and $u \ge u_{\min}$ since $\tau \ge 0$. So $\Omega$ also bounds $\omega^*(u)$,
and
$\Phi(y, p_2) \le (\Omega + \varepsilon_1) x/(p_1 p_2 \log p_2)$ for large $x$. The term is at most
\[
  (\Omega + \varepsilon_1) \, x \sum_{p_1 \in \mathcal P_i} \frac1{p_1} \sum_{p_2 \in \mathcal P_j}
  \frac1{p_2 \log p_2}.
\]
By the prime number theorem and partial summation, for large $x$,
\begin{gather*}
  \sum_{p \in \mathcal P_i} \frac1p \le \log\Bigl(1 + \frac h{e_i}\Bigr) + \varepsilon_1 \le t(e_i, h) + \varepsilon_1, \\
  \log x \sum_{p \in \mathcal P_j} \frac1{p \log p} \le \frac1{e_j} - \frac1{e_j + h} + \varepsilon_1
  = s(e_j, h) + \varepsilon_1.
\end{gather*}
These are the integrals of $d\alpha/\alpha$ and $d\alpha/\alpha^2$ over the cells; the first bound,
$\log(1 + h/e_i) \le t(e_i, h)$, is $\xi \le \sinh \xi$ at $\xi = \log(1 + h/e_i)$, since
$t(e, h) = \sinh \log(1 + h/e)$. Since
$\Delta_2(i, j)/\mathcal K \ge \Omega\, t(e_i, h)\, s(e_j, h)$, the term is at most
$(\Delta_2(i, j)/\mathcal K + \varepsilon_c) X$ once $\varepsilon_1$ is small.

(2) The same argument bounds a dropped counted cell $(i, j, k, l)$ of depth four, with
$\Phi(x/(p_1 \cdots p_4), p_4)$, three sums $\sum 1/p$ and one sum $\log x \sum 1/(p \log p)$, and a dropped counted
cell of depth six, whose last two primes lie in coarse cells. Here $y \ge 1$ follows, as in (1), from the test of
(O) at the lower corner of a counted cell, $e_i + e_j + e_k + 2e_l \le 1$ at depth four and
$e_i + e_j + e_k + e_l + e^C_b + 2e^C_c \le 1$ at depth six, with $4h \le v$ at depth four and $4h + 2h_C \le v$ at
depth six.

(3) A corner cell $(i, j)$ has $e_i + 2e_j > 1$, and its cell $i$ lies outside the window, since the term of depth one
above it is decomposed. Then $p_1 p_2^2 > 2x$ for large $x$, so the cofactor, which has no prime factor below $p_2$, is $1$,
and $n = p_1 p_2 > x$ with $p_1, p_2 < z_{\mathrm{top}}$. Both primes then lie in $(z_{\mathrm{top}}/4, z_{\mathrm{top}}]$,
so there are at most $(12\sqrt x/\log x)^2$ such integers for large $x$, and the corner cells together contribute
$O(x/\log^2 x) = o(X)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The refinement: keeping two-prime parts}

A dropped term $S(\mathcal A_{p_1 p_2}, p_2)$ counts, among others, the $n = p_1 p_2 \ell_1 \ell_2$ with primes
$p_2 < \ell_1 < \ell_2$. Such an $n$ is a product of four primes, and if some of their exponents sum into $\mathcal U$ it
belongs to a Type~II sum. The refinement keeps these parts instead of dropping them.

Let the cell $(i, j)$ be a candidate that is not decomposed, with $e_j + h \le e_i$ and $2(e_i + h) \le 1$, so that
$\alpha_2 < \alpha_1 < 1/2$ on its box. A cell $k$ is \emph{kept} below $(i, j)$ if $e_k \ge e_j + h$ (so $\ell_1 > p_2$
on the box), $2(e_k + h) + e_i + e_j + 2h \le 1$ (so that every prime $\ell_2$ with $p_1 p_2 \ell_1 \ell_2 \in (x, 2x]$
exceeds $\ell_1$), and the box $(i, j, k)$ is surely evaluable. The \emph{kept part} below $k$ counts the $n = p_1 p_2 \ell_1 \ell_2 \in (x, 2x]$ with $(p_1, p_2)$ in the
cell $(i, j)$, $\ell_1$ a prime of the cell $k$, $\ell_2 > \ell_1$ a prime, and $\ell_1, \ell_2 \ne p_1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:kept}
\begin{enumerate}
\item For each pair $(p_1, p_2)$ of the dropped term, the kept parts below the cell count distinct integers, all of
  which the term counts for that pair; so their $\mathcal A$-count is at most the term's.
\item Each kept part is the sum of three families of products of four primes in decreasing order, according to the
  position of $p_1$ among $\ell_1 < \ell_2$, and the exact-product case of Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}(b) evaluates each of
  them.
\item For every $\varepsilon_c > 0$ and all large $q$, the $\mathcal B$-version of the kept part below $k$ is at least
  $(\kappa(i, j, k)/\mathcal K - \varepsilon_c)\, x/\log x$, where
  $\kappa(i, j, k) = \lfloor 8\mathcal K h^3 / ((2e_i + h)(2e_j + h)(2e_k + h)(1 - e_i - e_j - e_k)) \rfloor$, so that
  $\kappa(i, j, k)/\mathcal K$ is a lower bound for the integral over the box of the density
  $d\alpha_1\, d\alpha_2\, d\beta/(\alpha_1 \alpha_2 \beta \gamma)$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ the exponents of $\ell_1$
  and $\ell_2$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $X = x/\log x$ and $\mathcal P_c$ for the set of primes of the cell $c$. By the definition of the term
$S(\mathcal A_{p_1 p_2}, p_2)$ of the cell $(i, j)$, its pairs $(p_1, p_2)$ have $p_2 < p_1$, $z_0 \le p_2$ and
$p_1 < z_{\mathrm{top}}$.

(1) Fix a pair $(p_1, p_2)$ of the term. An integer $n = p_1 p_2 \ell_1 \ell_2$ of a kept part determines
$\ell_1 \ell_2 = n/(p_1 p_2)$, hence the primes $\ell_1 < \ell_2$, and $\ell_1$ determines the cell $k$, since the cells
are disjoint. So, for this pair, the kept parts below $(i, j)$ count each integer at most once. Each such $n$ is counted
by the term for the pair: $n \in (x, 2x]$, $p_1 p_2 \mid n$, and every prime factor of $n$ is one of $p_1$, $p_2$,
$\ell_1$, $\ell_2$, which are all at least $p_2$, since $\ell_1 \ge x^{e_k} \ge x^{e_j + h} > p_2$ and $\ell_2 > \ell_1$.
Summing over the pairs, with the nonnegative weight $\mathbf 1[n \equiv a]$, gives (1). The kept $n$ are products of
four distinct primes; the integers of the term with a prime-power cofactor stay in the dropped remainder.

(2) For an integer $n = p_1 p_2 \ell_1 \ell_2$ of the kept part below $k$, exactly one of $\ell_2 < p_1$,
$\ell_1 < p_1 < \ell_2$ and $p_1 < \ell_1$ holds, since $\ell_1 < \ell_2$, $\ell_1, \ell_2 \ne p_1$ and $p_2 < \ell_1$.
So the kept part is the sum of three sums over the decreasing $4$-tuples $r_1 > r_2 > r_3 > r_4$ of primes, equal to
$(p_1, \ell_2, \ell_1, p_2)$, $(\ell_2, p_1, \ell_1, p_2)$ and $(\ell_2, \ell_1, p_1, p_2)$ respectively. The
conditions are $N_1 < r_1 r_2 r_3 r_4 \le N_2$, that $p_1$ lies in the cell $i$ with $z_0 \le p_1 < z_{\mathrm{top}}$,
that $p_2 = r_4$ lies in the cell $j$ with $p_2 \ge z_0$, and that $\ell_1$ lies in the cell $k$; $\ell_2$ is subject to
no cell condition. These, with the three of $r_1 > r_2 > r_3 > r_4$, are $14$ comparisons, with coefficients in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and constant terms
$O(\log q)$. Since the box $(i, j, k)$ is surely evaluable, the product of a set $T$ of the primes $p_1$, $p_2$,
$\ell_1$ lies, on the box, in $(x^{U_1/D}, x^{U_2/D})$ or in $(x^{1 - U_2/D}, x^{1 - U_1/D})$. In the first case
$m = \prod_T$ lies in the window's range; in the second the complementary product, which contains $\ell_2$, does, for
large $q$, as in the remark after Lemma~\ref{lem:chains}. Group the tuples by this $m$, a squarefree integer whose prime
factors, read in decreasing order, are the primes of its set. The comparisons that involve only these primes then
become conditions on $m$, absorbed into $a_m \in \{0, 1\}$, and the others are pivoted on the remaining primes. Each of
the three sums is then a sum as in Lemma~\ref{lem:family-sharp}, which bounds its $\mathcal A - \mathcal B/\varphi(q)$
by $q^{L - \delta}/\varphi(q)$.

(3) For $p_1 \in \mathcal P_i$, $p_2 \in \mathcal P_j$ and $\ell_1 \in \mathcal P_k$, put $y = x/(p_1 p_2 \ell_1)$ and
$\gamma_0 = 1 - e_i - e_j - e_k$. Since $\alpha_2 < \alpha_1 < 1/2$ on the box, $p_2 < p_1 < x^{1/2}$, so every such pair
belongs to the term. By the second condition of a kept cell, $\gamma_0 \ge e_k + 4h$ and $\ell_1 < y$, so every prime
$\ell_2 \in (y, 2y]$ has $\ell_2 > \ell_1$ and $p_1 p_2 \ell_1 \ell_2 \in (x, 2x]$. Exclude $\ell_2 = p_1$ and the
$\ell_2$ that divide $q$, at most $1 + \log_2 q$ primes, and exclude the triples with
$\ell_1 = p_1$ or with a prime dividing $q$, each of which has at most $\pi(2y) - \pi(y) \le y + 1$ primes $\ell_2$.
Since there are at most as many excluded triples as triples, the $\mathcal B$-version of the kept part is at least
\[
  \sum_{p_1, p_2, \ell_1} \bigl(\pi(2y) - \pi(y)\bigr) - (2 + \log_2 q) \#\{(p_1, p_2, \ell_1)\}
  - \sum_{\text{excluded triples}} y.
\]
On the box $x^{\gamma_0 - 3h} < y \le x^{\gamma_0}$, so for large $x$ the prime number theorem gives
$\pi(2y) - \pi(y) \ge (1 - \varepsilon) y/\log y \ge (1 - \varepsilon) y/(\gamma_0 \log x)$, and
\[
  \sum_{p_1, p_2, \ell_1} \frac y{\gamma_0 \log x} = \frac X{\gamma_0} \prod_{c \in \{i, j, k\}} \sum_{p \in \mathcal P_c} \frac1p
  \ge \frac X{\gamma_0} \Bigl(\prod_c \frac{2h}{2e_c + h} - 3d\Bigr),
\]
using $\sum_{p \in \mathcal P_c} 1/p \ge \log(1 + h/e_c) - d \ge 2h/(2e_c + h) - d$ for large $x$, which is the prime
number theorem again with $\log(1 + h/e_c) = 2\operatorname{artanh}(h/(2e_c + h)) \ge 2h/(2e_c + h)$, the first term of
the series, and that each sum is at most $1$, since it is at most $h/e_c + o(1) \le 1/4 + o(1)$ by $h \le e_c/4$. The excluded triples contribute $O(x \log q/z_0)$, since
$\sum_{p \ge z_0,\ p \mid q} 1/p \le \log_2 q/z_0$, and the triples number $O(x^{e_i + e_j + e_k + 3h})$ with
$e_i + e_j + e_k + 3h < 1$. Both are $o(X)$. Finally
$(1/\gamma_0) \prod_c 2h/(2e_c + h) \ge \kappa(i, j, k)/\mathcal K$, because the floor is at most the quotient. Taking
$\varepsilon$ and $d$ small in terms of $\varepsilon_c$ gives (3). The bound is the integral of the density over the box,
read from below: the number of $\ell_2$ is about $y/(\gamma_0 \log x)$, and $\int_e^{e + h} d\alpha/\alpha \ge 2h/(2e + h)$,
the value of $1/\alpha$ at the cell's midpoint times $h$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The certificate's total}

The certificate prices each candidate cell by what it costs: dropped, or decomposed with its own dropped terms.
\begin{itemize}
\item A counted cell $(i, j, k, l)$ of depth four costs
  $\Delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_4(i, j, k, l)$, the sum of the bounds $\Delta_6$ of the counted cells of depth six below its
  cells of depth five to which (E) and (O) do not apply, if it is decomposed, and its bound $\Delta_4(i, j, k, l)$
  otherwise. It is decomposed when the sharp rule allows it and $\Delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_4 \le \Delta_4$.
\item A candidate cell $(i, j)$ costs $\Delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_2(i, j)$, the sum over its cells of depth three to which (E)
  and (O) do not apply of the costs of their counted cells of depth four, if it is decomposed, and otherwise
  $\Delta_2(i, j)$ less $\kappa(i, j) = \sum_k \kappa(i, j, k)$, the sum over the cells $k$ kept below it (none unless
  $e_j + h \le e_i$ and $2(e_i + h) \le 1$), truncated at $0$. It is decomposed when the sharp rule allows it and
  $\Delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_2 \le \Delta_2$.
\item The total is the sum of the costs of the candidate cells, $\Sigma' = \sum_{i, j} \mathrm{cost}'(i, j)$.
\end{itemize}
On the grid of Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate} the choice at depth two is forced: the sharp rule allows $13230$ of the $27020$ candidate cells,
each of them has $\Delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_2 \le (\Delta_2 - \kappa(i, j))_+$, and so each is decomposed, and pricing every
candidate cell at the smaller of its two costs gives the same total. The choice at depth four is forced too: below the
decomposed candidate cells there are $2029388$ counted cells of depth four, the sharp rule allows $1441678$ of them, and
every one of these is decomposed (a computation, not formalised). Figure~\ref{fig:cells} shows the three kinds of
candidate cell.

The other cells of depth two cost nothing in the total: they are evaluated, empty, or corner cells
(Lemma~\ref{lem:drop}(3)). Without the kept parts, that is with $\kappa = 0$, the total $\Sigma$ is about
$1.12341\mathcal K$ on the grid of this paper; with the decomposition stopped at depth four it is about
$1.45721\mathcal K$ without the kept parts and $1.33134\mathcal K$ with them (computations, not formalised). The kept
parts remove about a ninth of the total. What lets the certificate close at $278/125$ is the decomposition's two
deeper levels, its kept parts, and the bounds of its cells, which take Buchstab's function itself on $[2, 3]$ and the
prime sums of the cells by the trapezoid rule.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:decomp-kept}
Assume the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2}. If $\Sigma'/\mathcal K \le c$, then for every $\varepsilon > 0$, for
all large $q$ and every $a$ prime to $q$,
\[
  S(\mathcal A, z_{\mathrm{top}}) \ge \frac{1}{\varphi(q)} S(\mathcal B, z_{\mathrm{top}})
  - (c + \varepsilon)\frac{x}{\varphi(q) \log x}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{The identities.} Buchstab's identity, the division of each prime's range into cells and the splits at $X'$ are
exact, so $S(\mathcal A, z_{\mathrm{top}})$ is the signed sum of the terms of the tree, and so is
$S(\mathcal B, z_{\mathrm{top}})$. Compare the two term by term. A dropped term has sign $+$ and an $\mathcal A$-count at
least $0$, and, for a dropped candidate cell with kept parts, at least the sum of the $\mathcal A$-counts of its kept
parts, by Lemma~\ref{lem:kept}(1). Every other term is evaluated or empty, or is decomposed. A decomposed term is the
signed sum of the terms below it, so one with sign $-$ is bounded from above, and one with sign $+$ from below, by
bounding those terms: the decomposed terms of sign $-$ are those of depths one, three and five, and those of sign $+$
the candidate cells of depth two and the counted cells of depth four that are decomposed.

\emph{The errors.} Let $\mathrm{Err} = \varepsilon_H x/(\varphi(q) \log q) + q^{L - \delta}/\varphi(q)$, with the $\varepsilon_H$ of
Proposition~\ref{prop:harman} small and the least of the $\delta$ of Lemmas~\ref{lem:family-sharp}
and~\ref{lem:chains} and Proposition~\ref{prop:harman}. Every evaluated term and every term $S(\cdot, z_0)$ has its $\mathcal A$- and
$\mathcal B/\varphi(q)$-counts within $\mathrm{Err}$, and every kept part within $3\,\mathrm{Err}$, by
Lemma~\ref{lem:kept}(2). There are at most $C_{\mathrm{dec}} = 1 + N(1 + N(2 + N(2 + N(N_C(N_C + 2) + 2))))$ terms,
and at most $N^3$ kept parts. So
\[
  S(\mathcal A, z_{\mathrm{top}}) \ge \frac{S(\mathcal B, z_{\mathrm{top}}) - (\mathrm{Dr}(\mathcal B) - \mathrm{Kp}(\mathcal B))}{\varphi(q)}
  - (C_{\mathrm{dec}} + 3N^3)\,\mathrm{Err},
\]
where $\mathrm{Dr}(\mathcal B)$ is the sum of the $\mathcal B$-versions of the dropped terms and $\mathrm{Kp}(\mathcal B)$
that of the kept parts. Taking $\varepsilon_H$ small in terms of $\varepsilon$ and $q$ large, the errors are below
$\varepsilon x/(2\varphi(q) \log x)$.

\emph{The dropped terms.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:drop}, a dropped candidate cell contributes at most
$(\Delta_2(i, j)/\mathcal K + \varepsilon_c) X$ to $\mathrm{Dr}(\mathcal B)$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:kept}(3) its kept parts
at least $(\kappa(i, j)/\mathcal K - N\varepsilon_c) X$ to $\mathrm{Kp}(\mathcal B)$; a decomposed candidate cell contributes at most
$(\Delta^{\mathrm{dec}}_2(i, j)/\mathcal K + N^2(1 + N_C^2)\varepsilon_c) X$, the sum over its dropped terms of depths four
and six; and the corner cells $o(X)$. Since truncating at $0$ can only raise a cost, each candidate cell contributes at
most $(\mathrm{cost}'(i, j)/\mathcal K + N^2(1 + N_C^2)\varepsilon_c) X$ to $\mathrm{Dr}(\mathcal B) - \mathrm{Kp}(\mathcal B)$.
Adding up and taking $\varepsilon_c$ small, $\mathrm{Dr}(\mathcal B) - \mathrm{Kp}(\mathcal B) \le (c + \varepsilon/2) X$ for
large $q$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The certificate}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:certificate}
On the grid of $N = 340$ cells a side, with $N_C = 85$ cells of four a side in the coarse grid, and with
$U_1 = \lceil (41556/100000) D \rceil$ and $U_2 = \lfloor (47531/100000) D \rfloor$, $\Sigma' < \mathcal K$.
\end{proposition}

The total is a finite sum of rational expressions in natural numbers, rounded to integers, so the proposition is a
computation. The formalisation checks it by evaluation in Lean's kernel, which gives
$\Sigma' \le 1151230848467235036$, that is $0.9985336\mathcal K$, below $0.99854\mathcal K$; the exact value is
$\Sigma' = 1150080767699533326 = 0.9975360\ldots\mathcal K$ (Section~\ref{sec:lean} and \S S5).

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}}\label{sec:proof}

The argument of Sections~\ref{sec:type1} and~\ref{sec:certificate} uses of the particular values of $L$, $u_1$, $u_2$
and $N$ only the conditions of the following theorem, of which Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is a case.

\begin{theorem}[the route theorem]\label{thm:route}
Let $2 < L \le 5/2$ and $0 < u_1 < u_2 < 1$, and let a grid of $N$ cells a side, with $4 \mid N$, integer ends $U_1$
and $U_2$ over $D = 2^{40}$ and its coarse grid of $N/4$ cells of four, satisfy $u_1 D < U_1$, $U_2 < u_2 D$ and
$L U_2 > D$, and the grid conditions
\[
  2U_2 < D, \quad 2e_{N - 1} < 1 < 2(v + Nh), \quad 16h \le v.
\]
Assume the conclusion of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2} for $L$ on the window $[u_1 L, u_2 L]$, and $\Sigma' < \mathcal K$ on
the grid.
Then there is $K'$ such that for every $q \ge 1$ and every $a$ prime to $q$ there is a prime $p \equiv a \pmod q$ with
$p \le K' q^L$.
\end{theorem}

The grid conditions are what the proofs of Sections~\ref{sec:certificate} and~\ref{sec:proof} use of the grid.
$2e_{N-1} < 1 < 2(v + Nh)$ gives $h > 0$, and then $16h \le v$ gives $v > 0$, that is $U_1 < U_2$; so the cells and the
floor $z_0 = \lceil x^v \rceil$ are well defined. $1 < 2(v + Nh)$ makes the cells, and the coarse cells, cover the
exponents of the primes below $z_{\mathrm{top}}$. $2e_{N-1} < 1$ gives $2e_i < 1$ for every cell, hence $v < 1/2$ and
$z_0 \le z_{\mathrm{top}}$; with $16h \le v$ it gives $v + Nh \le 1$, which keeps the thresholds of the comparisons of
every cell, the coarse ones included, in $[1, q^{L + 1}]$. $2U_2 < D$ gives $z_{\mathrm{top}} \le X'$. $16h \le v$ gives $y \ge 1$ in
Lemma~\ref{lem:drop} ($4h \le v$ at depths two and four, $12h \le v$ at depth six) and $h \le e_c/4$ in
Lemma~\ref{lem:kept}(3). Of the other conditions, $L U_2 > D$ gives $b_0 < L - 1$, and with $L \le 5/2$ it gives
$X'^{3/2} < N_1$ for the empty terms; $L \le 5/2$ also bounds the number of pieces in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:family-sharp}. The condition $2 < L$ follows from $L U_2 > D$ and $2U_2 < D$, and is used only through
$L > 1$.

\begin{proof}
Let $\rho = \Sigma'/\mathcal K < 1$ and $0 < \varepsilon < (1 - \rho)/4$. By the prime number theorem, for large $q$ the
primes in $(x, 2x]$ that do not divide $q$ number at least $(1 - \varepsilon) x/\log x$, and each of them is counted
by $S(\mathcal B, z_{\mathrm{top}})$. Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp-kept} gives, for large $q$ and every $a$ prime to
$q$,
\[
  S(\mathcal A, z_{\mathrm{top}}) \ge (1 - \varepsilon - \rho - \varepsilon) \frac{x}{\varphi(q) \log x}
  > \frac{1 - \rho}{2} \cdot \frac{x}{\varphi(q) \log x} > 0.
\]
An integer of $\mathcal A$ counted by $S(\mathcal A, z_{\mathrm{top}})$ is at most $N_2$ and has no prime factor at most
$N_2^{1/2}$, so it is a prime $p \equiv a \pmod q$ with $x < p \le 2q^L$. So there is $q_0$ such that the theorem holds
for $q \ge q_0$ with $K' = 2$. Each of the finitely many pairs $(q, a \bmod q)$ with $q < q_0$ has a prime
$p \equiv a \pmod q$ by Dirichlet's theorem, and $K'$ is taken large enough to cover them.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Take $L = 278/125$, $u_1 = 41556/100000$, $u_2 = 47531/100000$ and the grid of Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate}. Its
ends are rounded inward, $278 U_2 > 125 D$, and its grid conditions hold, by a computation in integers.
Theorem~\ref{thm:type2}, under
Inputs~\ref{inp:half-plane} and~\ref{inp:density}, is the Type~II estimate, and Proposition~\ref{prop:certificate} is
$\Sigma' < \mathcal K$. Theorem~\ref{thm:route} gives Theorem~\ref{thm:main} under the two inputs.
Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} enters through Lemma~\ref{lem:prime-sum} and the top bin in Section~\ref{sec:type2},
through the kernel bound of Section~\ref{sec:two-bounds} (where the Pólya--Vinogradov inequality would do instead),
through the piece of $g^*$ above $11/12$, and, in the formalisation, through the prime number theorem of
Sections~\ref{sec:type1} to~\ref{sec:proof}, which it derives from the case of $\zeta$ in the form
$\psi(t) - t = O(t^{11/12 + \varepsilon})$.
\end{proof}

The proof does not compute $K$: the threshold $q_0$ comes from limits in $q$ in each step, and the small moduli
from Dirichlet's theorem. As in the classical proofs, every constant is effective in principle; unlike them, the
argument needs no step on an exceptional zero, since Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} leaves none. But its thresholds are
enormous: the inward rounding of the window to multiples of $2^{-40}$, for one, leaves Proposition~\ref{prop:harman}
margins below $10^{-12}$ in its exponents, which only $\log q \gtrsim 10^{12}$ turns into a usable factor.

\section{The formalisation}\label{sec:lean}

Every numbered theorem, proposition, lemma and input of Sections~\ref{sec:intro} to~\ref{sec:proof}, and each result

\cite{repo}, which is built with Lean~4 and Mathlib, or, for Dirichlet's theorem, of Mathlib; Appendix~\ref{app:claims}
names them, and
\file{formalisations/} documents the results from the literature and the further classical results behind them. The
Lean statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is
{\small
\begin{verbatim}
theorem linnik_22240r : ∃ K : ℝ, ∀ (q : ℕ) [NeZero q] (a : ℕ),
    a.Coprime q → ∃ p : ℕ, p.Prime ∧ p ≡ a [MOD q] ∧
      (p : ℝ) ≤ K * (q : ℝ) ^ (278 / 125 : ℝ)
\end{verbatim}
}
where \lean{[NeZero q]} says $q \ge 1$ and the power is the real power. The file \texttt{lib/check/Check.lean} prints

dependency cone, so a \lean{sorry} anywhere in it would appear as \lean{sorryAx}; the library's own sources contain no

Lean \texttt{v4.35.0-rc3} with Mathlib at commit \texttt{0f64d30a}, and the repository's continuous integration builds
the library and checks the axioms on every change to it.

Independent checking of the inputs is limited. OpenAI's release has its own Lean formalisation of the main preprint's

\section{What the inputs give without the sieve}\label{sec:comparison}

This section claims nothing new. It places Theorem~\ref{thm:main} against what the same inputs give by the
classical argument, which has no sieve, and against the exponents known for special moduli; the table of the
introduction sums it up.

\subsection*{The classical deduction}

Suppose that for some $A \ge 2$ there is a uniform density estimate
\[
  N^*(\sigma, T; q) \le C (qT)^{A(1 - \sigma)} (\log qT)^B \qquad (1/2 \le \sigma \le 1,\ T \ge 2).
\]
With Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} it gives Linnik's theorem with every exponent $L > A$. The library's proof,
\lean{Linnik.linnik\_of\_charDensity}, writes the smoothed sum $\sum_{n \equiv a} \Lambda(n) e^{-n/x}$ through the
characters, bounds each non-trivial character's sum by a contour shift to the edge of its largest zero-free rectangle,
at one of the levels $\theta_j = \min(1/2 + j\delta/2, 11/12)$, for a small $\delta > 0$ fixed in terms of $L - A$,
and counts the characters at each level by the density
estimate at the height $T + 1$, with $T = 2 \log x + 2$: the characters with zeros of real part about $\sigma$
contribute about $(q^A/x)^{1 - \sigma}$ together, and none has a zero of real part above $11/12$. With it the library
proves:
\begin{itemize}
\item $L = 12 + \varepsilon$ from Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} alone: at most $\varphi(q) \le qT$ characters are
  counted for every $\sigma$, and at most one above $11/12$, which is the estimate with $A = 12$;
\item $L = 36/13 + \varepsilon$ with Ingham's estimate, whose exponent $3/(2 - \sigma)$ is $36/13$ at
  $\sigma = 11/12$;
\item $L = 12/5 + \varepsilon$ with Huxley's estimate (with Ingham's below $\sigma = 3/4$), which bounds the density
  exponent by $12/5$ on the whole range;
\item $L = 2.3334 + \varepsilon$ with the curve $g^*$, since $g^*(\sigma) \le 2.3334(1 - \sigma)$ on $[1/2, 1]$: on every
  step the exponent is at most $A_0 = 2.3334$, and $g^* = 1 \le 2.3334(1 - \sigma)$ for $\sigma < 4/7$, as
  $2.3334 \cdot 3/7 > 1$.
\end{itemize}
Appendix~\ref{app:claims} names the Lean theorem of each. With Chen, Gupta and Li's estimate itself the exponent is
$7/3 + \varepsilon$, by the same deduction from their Theorem~1.2 \cite{cgl} (exponent $A = 7/3$), which the library
does not formalise as they state it. They do not state a Linnik exponent, but their Corollary~1.4 \cite{cgl}, on primes
in progressions in short intervals under a zero-free region, contains this one: for $h \le x$ it gives
$\psi(x + h; q, a) - \psi(x; q, a) \sim h/\varphi(q)$ whenever
$h/\varphi(q) \ge x^{1/2 + \varepsilon} q^{1/6} + x^{17/30 + \varepsilon}$, and with $h = x$ and
Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} as the zero-free region this holds at $x = q^L$ for every $L > 7/3$ and all large $q$.


\subsection*{Special moduli}

For special moduli smaller exponents are known. For the powers of a fixed prime, Banks and Shparlinski
\cite{banks-shparlinski} have $2.1115$ (the value as cited in \cite[footnote~1]{cgl}). R.~Li \cite{li-harman} adapts
his Harman-sieve calculation to primes in progressions to large moduli $q$ whose prime factors are all below $q^C$, for
an absolute constant $C > 0$ whose existence his theorem asserts without giving it, when the $L$-functions of the
primitive characters to the divisors of $q$ have no zero with $\operatorname{Re} s > 1 - (\log q)^{-3/4}$ and
$|\operatorname{Im} s| \le \exp(\varepsilon (\log q)^{3/4})$, with the $\varepsilon > 0$ of his Theorem~9; for the
powers of a fixed prime he deduces the exponent $1/0.4752 < 2.1044$ (his Theorem~11), improving Banks and Shparlinski's.
He presents these theorems as modifications of that calculation, without separate proofs. Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}
implies the zero-free hypothesis of his Theorem~9 for every large $q$, so if that theorem holds as stated, it gives with
Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} the exponent $1/0.4752$ for every large modulus whose prime factors are all below $q^C$.

\subsection*{What the half-plane is used for}

Neither the classical deduction nor Theorem~\ref{thm:main} needs the value $11/12$ of Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}. The
deduction works with any zero-free half-plane $\operatorname{Re} s > 1 - \eta_0$, for a fixed $\eta_0 > 0$, in place of
Input~\ref{inp:half-plane}. In the sieve, the half-plane enters the window condition only at the top of the curve:
with Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} replaced by such a half-plane, the top piece $(101/50)(1 - \sigma)$ of $g^*$ extended up
to $1 - \eta_0$, and the margins of Definition~\ref{def:window} kept, the window of Theorem~\ref{thm:type2} stays
admissible at $278/125$ for every $\eta_0 \ge 2.85 \cdot 10^{-4}$. The binding corner is then $E_1$ at
$(1 - \eta_0, \mu_2)$; the corner of $E_2$ at $\mu = 1$ needs only $\eta_0 \ge 2.1 \cdot 10^{-4}$, and with smaller
margins any fixed $\eta_0 > 0$ would do. The other uses of Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} also need only some half-plane:
the contour of Lemma~\ref{lem:prime-sum}, the prime number theorem, and the kernel bound, for which the Pólya--Vinogradov
inequality would do (Section~\ref{sec:window-278}). So Theorem~\ref{thm:main} depends on OpenAI's result only through
the existence of some zero-free half-plane, that is, no zeros near $\operatorname{Re} s = 1$ and in particular no
exceptional zero. This is a remark, checked at the corners in exact rationals, not a theorem of the library, whose
statements use $\operatorname{Re} s > 11/12$.

\subsection*{Where the gain comes from, and its limits}

The deduction needs the zeros of every real part to contribute less than the main term, so its exponent is the
largest density exponent on $[1/2, 11/12]$, which is $7/3$, at $\sigma = 5/7$. The sieve needs the zeros only in
bilinear sums with one variable in the window, where Theorem~\ref{thm:type2} trades the density exponent against the
length of the variable, and recovers the primes from those sums at the cost that the certificate bounds; the window is
set at $5/7$, where the density exponent is largest (Section~\ref{sec:ends}). With steps of Ingham's and Huxley's
estimates, of length $1/1680$, in place of
Input~\ref{inp:density} on $[5/7, 131/168]$, the window is set at $3/4$, where both exponents are $12/5$, and a window
admissible at $L = 4537/2000 = 2.2685$ carries a certificate's total of $0.99835\mathcal K$; so the sieve brings
$12/5$ down to about $2.27$, with Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} as the only input that is not refereed. This is a
computation (\S S6), not a theorem of the library.

The method as it stands does not go much further. Of the total $0.99754\mathcal K$ at $278/125$, the decomposed cells
cost about $0.026\mathcal K$ and the dropped ones about $0.971\mathcal K$ (Figure~\ref{fig:cells}), of which about
$0.507\mathcal K$ comes from the cells that reach the line $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 1 - U_1/D$: most of their integers
have no product of some of their prime factors with its exponent in $\mathcal U$, whatever the factorisation of the
cofactor, and $p_1 p_2$ is beyond the Type~I level $X'$, so no refinement of the decomposition recovers most of that
part with this window (\S S6). More depth, a finer grid, sharper bounds for the cells and the half-plane
$\operatorname{Re} s > 7/8$ in place of Input~\ref{inp:half-plane} help little or not at all, and a smaller margin in
Definition~\ref{def:window} would gain about $10^{-4}$ in the exponent (\S S6); going lower needs a wider window, that is a better density exponent near $\sigma = 5/7$, or arithmetic information of
another kind. On the former, Chen, Gupta and Li note that improving the term of their estimate that dominates at
$\sigma = 5/7$ would need a joint cancellation which ``seems quite difficult'' \cite[Remark~12.3]{cgl}. \S S6 gives
these computations, and what lower density curves would give the method.
